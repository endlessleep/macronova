PROBLEM SET 3

Exercise 1

Study of an ARMA(2,1) Process

Consider the following process:

\[ X_t=2+\frac56X_{t-1}-\frac16X_{t-2} +\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}, \]

where \(\varepsilon_t\) is a zero-mean weak white noise with variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2>0\).

  1. Write the process using the polynomial operators.
  2. Show that \(X_t\) is stationary and that the representation is causal and fundamental.
  3. Give the AR(\(\infty\)) and MA(\(\infty\)) representations of \(X_t\).
  4. Determine the autocorrelation function of this process.
  5. Determine the first 3 terms of the partial autocorrelation function of this process.
  6. Determine the best linear forecast for \(h=1,2,3,\ldots,\infty\).
  7. Determine the forecast errors and their variance for \(h=1,2,3,\ldots,\infty\).
  8. Determine the prediction interval.

Question 1Lag polynomials and demeaned process

Formulas to use — Question 1

Lag operator:

\[L^jX_t=X_{t-j},\qquad L^k\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

Replace each lag by a power of \(L\).

AR polynomial:

\[\Phi(L)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jL^j.\]

Move the lagged \(X\) terms to the left, then factor out \(X_t\).

MA polynomial:

\[\Theta(L)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_kL^k.\]

Keep the shocks on the right, then factor out \(\varepsilon_t\). Read \(\phi_j\) and \(\theta_k\) from the original equation, keeping their signs.

Conclusion: identify \(\Phi(L)\) and \(\Theta(L)\), then write the equation using these two operators.

Solution

The process has intercept 2, AR coefficients \(5/6,-1/6\), and MA coefficient \(-1/7\).

We start with

\[ X_t=2+\frac56X_{t-1}-\frac16X_{t-2} +\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}. \]

1. Unconditional mean

For a stationary solution, let \(m_X=E(X_t)\). Taking expectations and using \(E(\varepsilon_t)=0\) gives

\[ m_X=2+\frac56m_X-\frac16m_X. \]

Rearranging,

\[ \left(1-\frac56+\frac16\right)m_X=2. \]
\[ \frac13m_X=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{m_X=6.} \]

2. Centred process

We set

\[ \widetilde X_t=X_t-m_X=X_t-6. \]

Substituting \(X_{t-j}=\widetilde X_{t-j}+6\) at each lag gives

\[\begin{aligned}\widetilde X_t+6&=2+\frac56(\widetilde X_{t-1}+6)\\&\quad-\frac16(\widetilde X_{t-2}+6)\\&\quad+\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}.\end{aligned}\]

After subtracting 6, the constant term is

\[2+\frac56\times6-\frac16\times6-6\]\[=2+5-1-6=0.\]

This is why the centred equation has no intercept:

\[ \boxed{ \widetilde X_t= \frac56\widetilde X_{t-1} -\frac16\widetilde X_{t-2} +\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}. } \]

Demeaning eliminates the intercept but does not change AR or MA coefficients. The new process has zero mean.

3. Lag polynomials

We have

\[ LX_t=X_{t-1},\qquad L^2X_t=X_{t-2}. \]

Moving the AR terms to the left gives

\[ \widetilde X_t -\frac56\widetilde X_{t-1} +\frac16\widetilde X_{t-2} =\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \left(1-\frac56L+\frac16L^2\right)\widetilde X_t =\left(1-\frac17L\right)\varepsilon_t. } \]

We set

\[ \boxed{\Phi(L)=1-\frac56L+\frac16L^2} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\Theta(L)=1-\frac17L.} \]

The centered polynomial representation is

\[ \boxed{\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.} \]

Equation in the original variable

Since \(\widetilde X_t=X_t-6\), the centred polynomial equation becomes

\[\Phi(L)(X_t-6)=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

The operator \(\Phi(L)\) acts on both \(X_t\) and the constant 6. A lag leaves a constant unchanged: \(L(6)=6\) and \(L^2(6)=6\). Therefore,

\[\begin{aligned}\Phi(L)(6)&=6-\frac56\times6+\frac16\times6\\&=6-5+1=2.\end{aligned}\]

By linearity,

\[\Phi(L)(X_t-6)=\Phi(L)X_t-\Phi(L)(6)\]\[=\Phi(L)X_t-2,\]\[\Phi(L)X_t-2=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

Thus the equation for the original observations \(X_t\) is

\[ \boxed{\Phi(L)X_t=2+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.} \]

6 is the mean of \(X_t\); 2 is the intercept of its ARMA equation. The equation in \(\widetilde X_t\) has intercept 0 because its mean has been removed. The two polynomial equations describe the same process using different variables.

Question 2Stationarity, causality and fundamentalness

Formulas to use — Question 2

AR — Roots and condition:

\[r^p\Phi(r^{-1})=0,\qquad |r_i|<1.\]

Solve the AR equation and check each inverse root. For an AR(2) with distinct inverse roots \(r_1,r_2\), factorize:

\[\Phi(L)=(1-r_1L)(1-r_2L).\]

AR — Inverse and series:

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\frac{A}{1-r_1L}+\frac{B}{1-r_2L},\]\[1=A(1-r_2L)+B(1-r_1L).\]

Match the constant and the coefficient of \(L\) to find \(A,B\). Expand each fraction as a geometric series:

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(A r_1^j+B r_2^j\right)L^j.\]

AR — Homogeneous solution:

\[\Phi(L)H_t=0,\qquad H_t=C_1r_1^t+C_2r_2^t.\]

For two distinct AR inverse roots, use the roots in the homogeneous solution. The constants \(C_1,C_2\) depend on the initial conditions. Check whether \(H_t\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\).

MA — Roots and condition:

\[s^q\Theta(s^{-1})=0,\qquad |s_i|<1.\]

Solve the MA equation and check each inverse root. For an MA(1), write its inverse root as \(s=-\theta_1\), then factorize:

\[\Theta(L)=1+\theta_1L=1-sL.\]

MA — Inverse and series:

\[\Theta(L)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-sL},\]\[\Theta(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}s^jL^j=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-\theta_1)^jL^j.\]

Use the geometric-series identity with \(s=-\theta_1\). Keep the sign inside the power when calculating the coefficients.

Conclusion — what each condition proves:

\[\forall i,\ |r_i|<1\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{stationary and causal},\]\[\forall i,\ |s_i|<1\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{fundamental}.\]

The first inverse expresses observations using current and past shocks; the second recovers shocks using current and past observations. Roots strictly inside the unit circle make these inverse-series coefficients absolutely summable.

Solution

From Question 1, \(\widetilde X_t=X_t-6\) and \(\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t\), with

\[\Phi(L)=1-\frac56L+\frac16L^2,\qquad \Theta(L)=1-\frac17L.\]

1. Stationarity and causality

The AR inverse roots satisfy

\[r^2\Phi(r^{-1})=0\]\[\Longleftrightarrow\ r^2-\frac56r+\frac16=0\]\[\Longleftrightarrow\ 6r^2-5r+1=(2r-1)(3r-1)=0.\]

Thus,

\[r_1=\frac12,\qquad r_2=\frac13,\qquad |r_1|<1,\quad |r_2|<1.\]

Both AR inverse roots have modulus less than 1, so the process admits a stationary and causal solution. The factorization is

\[\Phi(L)=\left(1-\frac12L\right)\left(1-\frac13L\right).\]

We decompose its inverse into partial fractions:

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\frac{A}{1-L/2}+\frac{B}{1-L/3}.\]

Multiplying by \((1-L/2)(1-L/3)\) gives

\[1=A\left(1-\frac13L\right)+B\left(1-\frac12L\right).\]

Identifying coefficients,

\[\begin{cases}A+B=1,\\\dfrac A3+\dfrac B2=0,\end{cases}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}A+B=1,\\2A+3B=0.\end{cases}\]

Subtracting twice the first equation from the second gives \(B=-2\), hence \(A=3\). Therefore,

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\frac{3}{1-L/2}-\frac{2}{1-L/3}\]\[=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left[3\left(\frac12\right)^j-2\left(\frac13\right)^j\right]L^j.\]

Writing \(a_j=3(1/2)^j-2(1/3)^j\), we obtain

\[\widetilde X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\left(\varepsilon_{t-j}-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-j-1}\right).\]

This representation uses only current and past shocks. Its coefficients are absolutely summable because both geometric ratios have modulus less than 1, which confirms stationarity and causality.

Associated homogeneous equation

The homogeneous part satisfies

\[H_t-\frac56H_{t-1}+\frac16H_{t-2}=0.\]

Substituting \(H_t=r^t\) gives the same characteristic equation:

\[r^2-\frac56r+\frac16=0.\]

Its two distinct roots are \(1/2\) and \(1/3\), so

\[\boxed{H_t=C_1\left(\frac12\right)^t+C_2\left(\frac13\right)^t.}\]

The constants \(C_1,C_2\) depend on the initial conditions. Since both roots have modulus less than 1,

\[\left(\frac12\right)^t\to0,\qquad\left(\frac13\right)^t\to0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H_t\to0.\]

Thus the effects of initial conditions vanish. The causal series derived above gives the stationary solution; adding a nonzero homogeneous term gives a transient that converges to zero.

2. Fundamentalness

The MA inverse root satisfies

\[s\Theta(s^{-1})=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s-\frac17=0.\]

Hence \(s=1/7\) and \(|s|<1\). We can therefore invert the MA polynomial:

\[\Theta(L)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-L/7}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jL^j.\]

Applying this inverse to the centred equation gives

\[\varepsilon_t=\Theta(L)^{-1}\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t\]\[=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^j\left(\widetilde X_{t-j}-\frac56\widetilde X_{t-j-1}+\frac16\widetilde X_{t-j-2}\right).\]

The coefficients are absolutely summable, and the expression contains only current and past observations. Thus \(\varepsilon_t\) can be recovered from the observed history: the representation is fundamental.

Conclusion

Since \(|1/2|<1\) and \(|1/3|<1\), the process has a stationary, causal solution. Since \(|1/7|<1\), its representation is also fundamental.

\[\boxed{X_t\text{ is stationary and causal; its representation is fundamental.}}\]

Question 3MA(∞) and AR(∞) representations

Formulas to use — Question 3

MA(∞) representation:

\[\widetilde X_t=\Phi(L)^{-1}\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Use the inverse from Question 2. Multiply by \(\Theta(L)\), then collect equal powers of \(L\) to find \(\psi_j\).

AR(∞) representation:

\[\varepsilon_t=\Theta(L)^{-1}\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t.\]

Multiply the MA inverse by \(\Phi(L)\), then collect equal powers of \(L\) to find the weights on the observations.

Return to the original variable:

\[X_t=m_X+\widetilde X_t.\]

Conclusion: write \(X_t\) in terms of shocks for MA(∞), and recover the current innovation from current and past observations for AR(∞).

Solution

From Question 2, \(\Phi(L)=(1-L/2)(1-L/3)\) and \(\Theta(L)=1-L/7\).

Part A — MA(∞) representation

From Question 1,

\[ \Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t. \]

Multiplying by the AR inverse gives

\[ \widetilde X_t=\Phi(L)^{-1}\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t. \]

Using Question 2,

\[ \widetilde X_t= \left[ 3\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac12\right)^jL^j -2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac13\right)^jL^j \right] \left(1-\frac17L\right)\varepsilon_t. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \widetilde X_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \left[ 3\left(\frac12\right)^j -2\left(\frac13\right)^j \right] \left(\varepsilon_{t-j}-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-j-1}\right). \]

We set

\[ a_j=3\left(\frac12\right)^j-2\left(\frac13\right)^j. \]

The first terms of the expansion are:

For \(j=0\),

\[ a_0\varepsilon_t-\frac17a_0\varepsilon_{t-1} =\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}. \]

For \(j=1\),

\[ a_1\varepsilon_{t-1}-\frac17a_1\varepsilon_{t-2}. \]

For \(j=2\),

\[ a_2\varepsilon_{t-2}-\frac17a_2\varepsilon_{t-3}. \]

Collecting terms with the same shock date gives

\[ \widetilde X_t =\varepsilon_t+ \left(a_1-\frac17a_0\right)\varepsilon_{t-1} +\left(a_2-\frac17a_1\right)\varepsilon_{t-2} +\cdots \]

Therefore the MA coefficients are

\[ \psi_0=1, \]

and for \(j\geq1\),

\[ \psi_j=a_j-\frac17a_{j-1}. \]

Substituting the expression for \(a_j\) gives

\[ \psi_j= 3\left(\frac12-\frac17\right) \left(\frac12\right)^{j-1} - 2\left(\frac13-\frac17\right) \left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \psi_j= \frac{15}{14}\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1} -\frac8{21}\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}, \quad j\ge1. } \]

The first coefficients are

\[ \psi_0=1,\quad \psi_1=\frac{29}{42},\quad \psi_2=\frac{103}{252},\quad \psi_3=\frac{341}{1512}. \]

The final MA(∞) representation is

\[ \boxed{ X_t=6+\varepsilon_t+ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \left[ \frac{15}{14}\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1} -\frac8{21}\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1} \right]\varepsilon_{t-j}. } \]

The first terms are therefore

\[ X_t=6+\varepsilon_t +\frac{29}{42}\varepsilon_{t-1} +\frac{103}{252}\varepsilon_{t-2} +\frac{341}{1512}\varepsilon_{t-3} +\cdots \]

The causal MA(∞) form uses current and past shocks only, and its coefficients decay toward zero.

Part B — AR(∞) representation

We again have

\[ \Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t. \]

Multiplying by the MA inverse gives

\[ \varepsilon_t=\Theta(L)^{-1}\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t. \]

Using

\[ \Theta(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jL^j, \]

we obtain

\[ \varepsilon_t= \left(1-\frac56L+\frac16L^2\right) \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^j \widetilde X_{t-j}. \]

The first terms of the expansion are:

For \(j=0\),

\[ \widetilde X_t-\frac56\widetilde X_{t-1} +\frac16\widetilde X_{t-2}. \]

For \(j=1\),

\[ \frac17\widetilde X_{t-1} -\frac5{42}\widetilde X_{t-2} +\frac1{42}\widetilde X_{t-3}. \]

For \(j=2\),

\[ \frac1{49}\widetilde X_{t-2} -\frac5{294}\widetilde X_{t-3} +\frac1{294}\widetilde X_{t-4}. \]

Collecting the coefficients at each lag gives:

Coefficient of \(\widetilde X_{t-1}\):

\[ \frac17-\frac56=-\frac{29}{42}. \]

Coefficient of \(\widetilde X_{t-2}\):

\[ \frac1{49}-\frac5{42}+\frac16 =\frac{10}{147}. \]

More generally, for \(j\ge2\), the coefficient of \(\widetilde X_{t-j}\) is

\[ \frac{10}{147}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-2}. \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ \varepsilon_t= \widetilde X_t-\frac{29}{42}\widetilde X_{t-1} +\sum_{j=2}^{\infty} \frac{10}{147}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-2} \widetilde X_{t-j}. } \]

In original variables, the same expression is

\[ \boxed{ \varepsilon_t= -\frac73+X_t-\frac{29}{42}X_{t-1} +\sum_{j=2}^{\infty} \frac{10}{147}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-2}X_{t-j}. } \]

The constant \(-7/3=-2/\Theta(1)\) arises when using uncentered observations.

Question 4Autocorrelation function

Formulas to use — Question 4

Yule–Walker equations for ARMA(2,1):

\[\begin{aligned}\gamma(0)&=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(2)\\&\quad+\sigma_\varepsilon^2[1+\theta_1(\phi_1+\theta_1)],\end{aligned}\]\[(1-\phi_2)\gamma(1)=\phi_1\gamma(0)+\theta_1\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\]\[\gamma(2)=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(0).\]

Insert the AR and MA coefficients, then solve for \(\gamma(0),\gamma(1),\gamma(2)\), the autocovariances at lags 0, 1 and 2.

Higher lags:

\[\gamma(h)=\phi_1\gamma(h-1)+\phi_2\gamma(h-2),\qquad h\ge2.\]

Apply the recurrence using the initial autocovariances.

Autocorrelation:

\[\rho(h)=\frac{\gamma(h)}{\gamma(0)}.\]

Conclusion: divide each autocovariance by the variance \(\gamma(0)\) to obtain the ACF.

Solution

The innovations are uncorrelated, with mean zero and variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). From Question 3, the MA(∞) weight at lag one is \(\psi_1=29/42\).

The centred model is

\[ \widetilde X_t= \phi_1\widetilde X_{t-1} +\phi_2\widetilde X_{t-2} +\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}, \]

where

\[ \phi_1=\frac56,\qquad \phi_2=-\frac16,\qquad \theta_1=-\frac17. \]

We have

\[ \gamma(h)=E[\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t-h}], \qquad \rho(h)=\frac{\gamma(h)}{\gamma(0)}. \]

1. \(\gamma(0)\)

Multiplying the centred equation by \(\widetilde X_t\) and taking expectations gives

\[ \gamma(0)=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(2) +E[\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_t] +\theta_1E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\widetilde X_t]. \]

Because the process is causal,

\[ E[\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_t]=\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \]

and

\[ E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\widetilde X_t] =(\phi_1+\theta_1)\sigma_\varepsilon^2. \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ \gamma(0)= \phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(2) +\sigma_\varepsilon^2[1+\theta_1(\phi_1+\theta_1)]. } \]

2. \(\gamma(1)\)

Multiplying by \(\widetilde X_{t-1}\) and taking expectations gives

\[ \gamma(1)=\phi_1\gamma(0)+\phi_2\gamma(1) +\theta_1\sigma_\varepsilon^2. \]

Rearranging,

\[ \boxed{ \gamma(1)= \frac{\phi_1\gamma(0)+\theta_1\sigma_\varepsilon^2} {1-\phi_2}. } \]

3. \(\gamma(2)\)

We multiply the equation by \(\widetilde X_{t-2}\) and take expectations.

The innovation terms have zero covariance with this older observation, so

\[ \boxed{ \gamma(2)=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(0). } \]

4. Solution of the system

Setting \(g_h=\gamma(h)/\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), the three equations become

\[ g_0=\frac56g_1-\frac16g_2+\frac{265}{294},\qquad g_1=\frac57g_0-\frac6{49},\qquad g_2=\frac56g_1-\frac16g_0. \]

Substituting the third equation into the first, then using the second, gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{35}{36}g_0&=\frac{25}{36}g_1+\frac{265}{294},\\ 35g_0&=25\left(\frac57g_0-\frac6{49}\right)+\frac{1590}{49},\\ \frac{120}{7}g_0&=\frac{1440}{49},\qquad g_0=\frac{12}{7}. \end{aligned} \]
\[ g_1=\frac{60}{49}-\frac6{49}=\frac{54}{49},\qquad g_2=\frac{45}{49}-\frac{14}{49}=\frac{31}{49}. \]

With \(\phi_1=5/6,\phi_2=-1/6,\theta_1=-1/7\), we therefore obtain

\[ \boxed{\gamma(0)=\frac{12}{7}\sigma_\varepsilon^2} \]
\[ \boxed{\gamma(1)=\frac{54}{49}\sigma_\varepsilon^2} \]
\[ \boxed{\gamma(2)=\frac{31}{49}\sigma_\varepsilon^2}. \]

5. higher lags

Since \(q=1\), for \(h>1\) the autocovariances satisfy the AR(2) recurrence:

\[ \boxed{ \gamma(h)=\frac56\gamma(h-1)-\frac16\gamma(h-2), \quad h\ge2. } \]

For \(h=3\),

\[ \gamma(3)=\frac56\gamma(2)-\frac16\gamma(1). \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{\gamma(3)=\frac{101}{294}\sigma_\varepsilon^2}. \]

6. Autocorrelation function

Dividing by \(\gamma(0)\) gives

\[ \boxed{\rho(0)=1} \]
\[ \boxed{\rho(1)=\frac9{14}\approx0.642857} \]
\[ \boxed{\rho(2)=\frac{31}{84}\approx0.369048} \]
\[ \boxed{\rho(3)=\frac{101}{504}\approx0.200397}. \]

For all \(h\ge2\),

\[ \boxed{ \rho(h)=\frac56\rho(h-1)-\frac16\rho(h-2). } \]

Solving this recurrence using its two characteristic roots gives

\[ \boxed{ \rho(h)=\frac{13}{7}\left(\frac12\right)^h -\frac67\left(\frac13\right)^h, \quad h\ge0. } \]

The ACF tails off rather than cutting off, because this is a mixed ARMA model.

For negative lags, symmetry gives \(\gamma(-h)=\gamma(h)\) and \(\rho(-h)=\rho(h)\).

Question 5First three terms of the PACF

Formulas to use — Question 5

Correlation matrix:

\[(R_h)_{ij}=\rho(|i-j|),\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,h,\]\[\boldsymbol\rho_h=(\rho(1),\ldots,\rho(h))^{\mathsf T}.\]

Use the ACF from Question 4 to build \(R_h\) for each lag \(h=1,2,3\).

Cramer’s rule for the last projection coefficient:

\[\alpha(h)=\frac{\det(R_h^\star)}{\det(R_h)}.\]

Replace the last column of \(R_h\) by \(\boldsymbol\rho_h\) to obtain \(R_h^\star\). Calculate both determinants, then divide.

Conclusion: report \(\alpha(1),\alpha(2),\alpha(3)\), the first three PACF terms.

Solution

From Question 4, the first three autocorrelations determine the projection equations.

We solve the projection equations by Cramer’s rule.

We already know

\[ \rho(1)=\frac9{14},\qquad \rho(2)=\frac{31}{84},\qquad \rho(3)=\frac{101}{504}. \]

The PACF at lag \(h\) is the last coefficient in the linear projection of \(\widetilde X_t\) onto its first \(h\) lags.

Let \(R_h\) be the autocorrelation matrix and \(R_h^\star\) the same matrix with its last column replaced by the vector of autocorrelations.

Then

\[ \boxed{ \alpha(h)=\frac{\det(R_h^\star)}{\det(R_h)}. } \]

Lag 1

\[ \boxed{ \alpha(1)=\rho(1)=\frac9{14}\approx0.642857. } \]

Lag 2

\[ R_2= \begin{pmatrix} 1&\rho(1)\\ \rho(1)&1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Replacing the second column gives

\[ R_2^\star= \begin{pmatrix} 1&\rho(1)\\ \rho(1)&\rho(2) \end{pmatrix}. \]

By Cramer's rule,

\[ \alpha(2)= \frac{ \begin{vmatrix} 1&\rho(1)\\ \rho(1)&\rho(2) \end{vmatrix} }{ \begin{vmatrix} 1&\rho(1)\\ \rho(1)&1 \end{vmatrix} }. \]

Expanding the determinants gives

\[ \alpha(2)= \frac{\rho(2)-\rho(1)^2}{1-\rho(1)^2}. \]

Writing \(a=9/14\) and \(b=31/84\),

\[ b-a^2=-\frac{13}{294},\qquad 1-a^2=\frac{115}{196},\qquad \frac{-13/294}{115/196}=-\frac{26}{345}. \]

Substituting,

\[ \boxed{ \alpha(2)=-\frac{26}{345}\approx-0.075362. } \]

Lag 3

The original autocorrelation matrix is

\[ R_3= \begin{pmatrix} 1&\rho(1)&\rho(2)\\ \rho(1)&1&\rho(1)\\ \rho(2)&\rho(1)&1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Replacing the last column gives

\[ R_3^\star= \begin{pmatrix} 1&\rho(1)&\rho(1)\\ \rho(1)&1&\rho(2)\\ \rho(2)&\rho(1)&\rho(3) \end{pmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \alpha(3)=\frac{\det(R_3^\star)}{\det(R_3)}. } \]

With \(a=9/14,\ b=31/84,\ c=101/504\),

Expanding both three-by-three determinants along the first row gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \det(R_3)&=(1-a^2)-a(a-ab)+b(a^2-b)\\ &=1-2a^2+2a^2b-b^2\\ &=1-\frac{81}{98}+\frac{837}{2744}-\frac{961}{7056}\\ &=\frac{16907}{49392},\\ \det(R_3^\star)&=(c-ab)-a(ac-b^2)+a(a^2-b)\\ &=c(1-a^2)-2ab+ab^2+a^3\\ &=\frac{11615-46872+8649+26244}{98784}\\ &=-\frac{13}{3528}. \end{aligned} \]

After inserting the exact ACF coefficients,

\[ \det(R_3^\star)=-\frac{13}{3528}, \]
\[ \det(R_3)=\frac{16907}{49392}. \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ \alpha(3)=-\frac{182}{16907}\approx-0.010765. } \]

Final PACF results

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \alpha(1)&\approx0.642857,\\ \alpha(2)&\approx-0.075362,\\ \alpha(3)&\approx-0.010765. \end{aligned} } \]

The PACF of a mixed ARMA(2,1) generally tails off. It does not necessarily become exactly zero after lag 2.

Question 6Best linear forecast for all horizons

Formulas to use — Question 6

Best linear forecast from the MA(∞) representation:

\[\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=m_X+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}.\]

Insert the mean and MA weights found earlier. Keep shocks dated at or before \(t\); replace future shocks by zero.

Recursive forecasts from the original equation:

\[\begin{aligned}\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}&=c+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i X^*_{t+h-i\mid t}\\&\quad+\sum_{k=h}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{t+h-k},\end{aligned}\]\[X^*_{t+s\mid t}=\begin{cases}X_{t+s},&s\le0,\\\widehat X_{t+s\mid t},&s>0.\end{cases}\]

Keep the intercept \(c\) from the original equation. Use observed values for past dates and previously calculated forecasts for future dates. An empty MA sum is zero.

Conclusion: calculate horizons 1, 2 and 3, write the general forecast, then take its limit as \(h\to\infty\).

Solution

We forecast from date \(t\), using the observed history and treating the parameters as known.

Since the representation is fundamental, shocks dated at or before \(t\) are recoverable from the observed history. Future shocks have zero best linear forecast.

From Question 3,

\[ X_t=6+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}. \]

Thus

\[ X_{t+h} =6+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}. \]

Separating future shocks from shocks dated at or before \(t\) gives

\[ X_{t+h} =6+ \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j} + \sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}. \]

The first sum contains future shocks, whose best linear forecasts are zero.

Therefore the best linear forecast is

\[ \boxed{ \widehat X_{t+h\mid t} =6+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}. } \]

Equivalently, after reindexing,

\[ \boxed{ \widehat X_{t+h\mid t} =6+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_{h+k}\varepsilon_{t-k}. } \]

Horizon \(h=1\)

Using the original ARMA equation and setting the forecast of \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) to zero,

\[ \boxed{ \widehat X_{t+1\mid t} =2+\frac56X_t-\frac16X_{t-1}-\frac17\varepsilon_t. } \]

The current shock \(\varepsilon_t\) is recoverable from the observed history by fundamentalness, whereas \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) is unknown.

Horizon \(h=2\)

At date \(t+2\), the model is

\[ X_{t+2} =2+\frac56X_{t+1}-\frac16X_t +\varepsilon_{t+2}-\frac17\varepsilon_{t+1}. \]

Both future shocks have zero best linear forecast. The unknown observation \(X_{t+1}\) is replaced with its forecast:

\[ \boxed{ \widehat X_{t+2\mid t} =2+\frac56\widehat X_{t+1\mid t}-\frac16X_t. } \]

Horizon \(h=3\)

\[ \boxed{ \widehat X_{t+3\mid t} =2+\frac56\widehat X_{t+2\mid t} -\frac16\widehat X_{t+1\mid t}. } \]

General horizon

For \(h\ge2\),

\[ \boxed{ \widehat X_{t+h\mid t} =2+\frac56\widehat X_{t+h-1\mid t} -\frac16\widehat X_{t+h-2\mid t}, } \]

with initial values

\[ \widehat X_{t\mid t}=X_t \]

and the one-step forecast calculated above.

This recursion is applied for \(h\ge2\); the one-step forecast includes the known shock \(\varepsilon_t\).

Infinite horizon

As \(h\to\infty\), the stable AR roots imply that the effects of today's information disappear.

The forecast approaches the unconditional mean:

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{h\to\infty}\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=m_X=6. } \]

Thus the best linear forecast converges in mean square to the mean \(m_X=6\).

Question 7Forecast errors and their variances

Formulas to use — Question 7

Forecast error:

\[e_t(h)=X_{t+h}-\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}.\]

Subtract the forecast from the MA representation. Keep only the future shocks.

Forecast-error variance:

\[\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2.\]

Use the weights from Question 3. Square the first \(h\) weights, add them, then multiply by the innovation variance.

Geometric sums:

\[\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}a^j=\frac{1-a^h}{1-a},\qquad a\ne1.\]

Expand \(\psi_j^2\), then sum each geometric term. For the infinite horizon, take the limit of the variance as \(h\to\infty\).

Conclusion: report the error and its variance for each finite horizon, then the limiting error variance.

Solution

From Questions 3 and 6, the forecast error is obtained by subtracting the forecast from the MA representation.

1. Forecast error definition

We set

\[ \boxed{ e_t(h)=X_{t+h}-\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}. } \]

Using the MA(∞) representation from Question 3 and the forecast from Question 6,

\[ e_t(h)= \left[6+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}\right] - \left[6+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}\right]. \]

The already-known terms cancel.

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ e_t(h)=\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}. } \]

The forecast error contains only future shocks, unknown when the forecast was made.

2. First forecast errors

For \(h=1\):

\[ \boxed{ e_t(1)=\varepsilon_{t+1}. } \]

For \(h=2\):

\[ e_t(2)=\varepsilon_{t+2}+\psi_1\varepsilon_{t+1}. \]

Using \(\psi_1=29/42\):

\[ \boxed{ e_t(2)=\varepsilon_{t+2} +\frac{29}{42}\varepsilon_{t+1}. } \]

For \(h=3\):

\[ \boxed{ e_t(3)= \varepsilon_{t+3} +\frac{29}{42}\varepsilon_{t+2} +\frac{103}{252}\varepsilon_{t+1}. } \]

3. Forecast error variance

White-noise shocks have constant variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) and are uncorrelated across dates.

Hence the variance of the weighted sum is the sum of its squared coefficients multiplied by \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\):

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)] =\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2. } \]

For \(h=1\):

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(1)]=\sigma_\varepsilon^2. } \]

For \(h=2\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(2)] =\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \left[1+\left(\frac{29}{42}\right)^2\right]. \]
\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(2)] =\frac{2605}{1764}\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \approx1.476757\sigma_\varepsilon^2. } \]

For \(h=3\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(3)] =\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \left[ 1+\left(\frac{29}{42}\right)^2 +\left(\frac{103}{252}\right)^2 \right]. \]
\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(3)] =\frac{104389}{63504}\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \approx1.643818\sigma_\varepsilon^2. } \]

4. General horizon

We have

\[ \psi_0=1, \]

and, for \(j\ge1\),

\[ \psi_j= \frac{15}{14}\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1} -\frac8{21}\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}. \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)] =\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \left[ 1+ \sum_{j=1}^{h-1} \left( \frac{15}{14}\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1} -\frac8{21}\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1} \right)^2 \right]. } \]

Summing the squared coefficients

Writing \(V_h=\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]\) and \(n=j-1\), squaring the difference gives

\[ \psi_j^2=\frac{225}{196}\left(\frac14\right)^n +\frac{64}{441}\left(\frac19\right)^n -\frac{40}{49}\left(\frac16\right)^n. \]

Applying \(\sum_{n=0}^{h-2}r^n=(1-r^{h-1})/(1-r)\), with an empty sum for \(h=1\), gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{V_h}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2} &=1+\frac{75}{49}[1-(1/4)^{h-1}]\\ &\quad+\frac8{49}[1-(1/9)^{h-1}]\\ &\quad-\frac{48}{49}[1-(1/6)^{h-1}]. \end{aligned} \]

The constant is \(1+75/49+8/49-48/49=12/7\).

Summing the three geometric series gives the closed form valid for integer \(h\ge1\):

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)] =\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \left[ \frac{12}{7} -\frac{75}{49}\left(\frac14\right)^{h-1} -\frac8{49}\left(\frac19\right)^{h-1} +\frac{48}{49}\left(\frac16\right)^{h-1} \right]. } \]

5. Infinite horizon

Since \(1/4\), \(1/9\) and \(1/6\) are all strictly smaller than 1, their powers converge to zero as \(h\to\infty\).

Consequently,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{h\to\infty}\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)] =\frac{12}{7}\sigma_\varepsilon^2. } \]

This equals the unconditional variance \(\gamma(0)\) found in Question 4.

Question 8Prediction interval

Formulas to use — Question 8

Prediction interval under iid Gaussian innovations:

\[PI_{1-\alpha}(h)=\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]}.\]

Use the forecast from Question 6 and the error variance from Question 7. Choose the standard-normal quantile \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) for coverage \(1-\alpha\).

For 95% coverage:

\[z_{0.975}\simeq1.96.\]

Subtract the margin for the lower endpoint; add it for the upper endpoint.

Conclusion: give both endpoints for the requested horizons, stating the Gaussian assumption.

Solution

The forecast and its error variance are known from Questions 6 and 7; Gaussianity is an additional assumption.

1. Distribution of the forecast error

To construct a normal prediction interval, we additionally assume

\[ \varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2). \]

We give the general \(1-\alpha\) level and illustrate it at 95%.

2. Prediction interval

Under Gaussian innovations, the forecast error is normally distributed with mean zero and variance

\[ V_h=\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]. \]

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) prediction interval is

\[ \boxed{ PI_{1-\alpha}(h)= \left[ \widehat X_{t+h\mid t} -z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{V_h}, \quad \widehat X_{t+h\mid t} +z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{V_h} \right]. } \]

Here, \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) is the corresponding standard-normal quantile.

For a 95% prediction interval,

\[ z_{0.975}\approx1.96. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{ PI_{95\%}(h)= \widehat X_{t+h\mid t} \pm1.96\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]}. } \]

Substituting Question 7:

\[ \boxed{ PI_{95\%}(h)= \widehat X_{t+h\mid t} \pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon \sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2}. } \]

3. First horizons

For \(h=1\):

\[ \boxed{ PI_{95\%}(1)= \widehat X_{t+1\mid t} \pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon. } \]

For \(h=2\):

\[ \boxed{ PI_{95\%}(2)= \widehat X_{t+2\mid t} \pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon \sqrt{1+\left(\frac{29}{42}\right)^2}. } \]

For \(h=3\):

\[ \boxed{ PI_{95\%}(3)= \widehat X_{t+3\mid t} \pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon \sqrt{ 1+\left(\frac{29}{42}\right)^2 +\left(\frac{103}{252}\right)^2 }. } \]

4. Infinite horizon

From Questions 6 and 7,

\[ \widehat X_{t+h\mid t}\longrightarrow6 \]

and

\[ \operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)] \longrightarrow\frac{12}{7}\sigma_\varepsilon^2. \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ PI_{95\%}(\infty) =6\pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{12}{7}}. } \]

The interval widens with the horizon and approaches this limiting interval. These normal prediction intervals use the additional iid Gaussian assumption; weak white noise alone does not justify normal coverage.
