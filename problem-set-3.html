PROBLEM SET 3

Exercise 1

Study of an ARMA(2,1) Process

Consider the following process:

\[ X_t=2+\frac56X_{t-1}-\frac16X_{t-2} +\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}, \]

where \(\varepsilon_t\) is a zero-mean weak white noise with variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2>0\).

  1. Write the process using the polynomial operators.
  2. Show that \(X_t\) is stationary and that the representation is causal and fundamental.
  3. Give the AR(\(\infty\)) and MA(\(\infty\)) representations of \(X_t\).
  4. Determine the autocorrelation function of this process.
  5. Determine the first 3 terms of the partial autocorrelation function of this process.
  6. Determine the best linear forecast for \(h=1,2,3,\ldots,\infty\).
  7. Determine the forecast errors and their variance for \(h=1,2,3,\ldots,\infty\).
  8. Determine the prediction interval.

Question 1Lag polynomials and centred process

Formulas to use — Question 1

Lag operator:

\[L^jX_t=X_{t-j},\qquad L^k\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

Replace each lag by a power of \(L\).

AR polynomial:

\[\Phi(L)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jL^j.\]

Move the lagged \(X\) terms to the left, then factor out \(X_t\).

MA polynomial:

\[\Theta(L)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_kL^k.\]

Keep the shocks on the right, then factor out \(\varepsilon_t\). Read \(\phi_j\) and \(\theta_k\) from the original equation, keeping their signs.

Conclusion: identify \(\Phi(L)\) and \(\Theta(L)\), then write the equation using these two operators.

Solution

We first centre the process to remove its constant. For a stationary solution, the mean is the same at every date: \(E[X_t]=E[X_{t-1}]=E[X_{t-2}]=m_X\). The innovations have mean zero, so taking expectations of the original equation gives

\[m_X=2+\frac56m_X-\frac16m_X.\]

Collect the terms in \(m_X\) on the left, then divide by their coefficient:

\[\left(1-\frac56+\frac16\right)m_X=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_X=\frac{2}{1-\frac56+\frac16}=6.\]

Define \(\widetilde X_t=X_t-6\). Substitute \(X_s=\widetilde X_s+6\) at each date \(s\):

\[\widetilde X_t+6=2+\frac56(\widetilde X_{t-1}+6)-\frac16(\widetilde X_{t-2}+6)+\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}.\]

The constants on the right add up to \(2+5-1=6\), which cancels the 6 on the left. This gives the mean-deviation equation:

\[\widetilde X_t=\frac56\widetilde X_{t-1}-\frac16\widetilde X_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}.\]

Move the two lagged observations to the left; their signs change:

\[\widetilde X_t-\frac56\widetilde X_{t-1}+\frac16\widetilde X_{t-2}=\varepsilon_t-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}.\]

Now use \(L\widetilde X_t=\widetilde X_{t-1}\), \(L^2\widetilde X_t=\widetilde X_{t-2}\), and \(L\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Factoring out the current variable on each side gives

\[\underbrace{\left(1-\frac56L+\frac16L^2\right)}_{\Phi(L)}\widetilde X_t=\underbrace{\left(1-\frac17L\right)}_{\Theta(L)}\varepsilon_t.\]

Thus the two lag polynomials are

\[\Phi(L)=1-\frac56L+\frac16L^2,\qquad\Theta(L)=1-\frac17L.\]

We will also use the original-variable equation in Question 3:

\[\Phi(L)X_t=2+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

The 2 here is the original intercept. Centring removes it because \(\Phi(L)6=6(1-5/6+1/6)=2\). Stationarity is checked in Question 2.

Question 2Stationarity, causality and fundamentalness

Formulas to use — Question 2

AR — Roots and condition:

\[r^p\Phi(r^{-1})=0,\qquad |r_i|<1.\]

Solve the AR equation and check each inverse root. For an AR(2) with distinct inverse roots \(r_1,r_2\), factorize:

\[\Phi(L)=(1-r_1L)(1-r_2L).\]

AR — Inverse and series:

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\frac{A}{1-r_1L}+\frac{B}{1-r_2L},\]\[1=A(1-r_2L)+B(1-r_1L).\]

Match the constant and the coefficient of \(L\) to find \(A,B\). Expand each fraction as a geometric series:

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(A r_1^j+B r_2^j\right)L^j.\]

MA — Roots and condition:

\[s^q\Theta(s^{-1})=0,\qquad |s_i|<1.\]

Solve the MA equation and check each inverse root. For an MA(1), write its inverse root as \(s=-\theta_1\), then factorize:

\[\Theta(L)=1+\theta_1L=1-sL.\]

MA — Inverse and series:

\[\Theta(L)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-sL},\]\[\Theta(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}s^jL^j=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-\theta_1)^jL^j.\]

Use the geometric-series identity with \(s=-\theta_1\). Keep the sign inside the power when calculating the coefficients.

Conclusion — what each condition proves:

\[\forall i,\ |r_i|<1\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{stationary and causal},\]\[\forall i,\ |s_i|<1\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{fundamental}.\]

The first inverse expresses observations using current and past shocks; the second recovers shocks using current and past observations. Roots strictly inside the unit circle make these inverse-series coefficients absolutely summable.

Solution

For the AR part, we check stationarity and causality by computing the inverse roots. The characteristic equation is:

\[z^2\Phi(z^{-1})=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z^2-\frac56z+\frac16=0.\]

To obtain this equation, replace \(L\) by \(z^{-1}\), then multiply by \(z^2\) to remove the negative powers:

\[z^2\left(1-\frac56z^{-1}+\frac16z^{-2}\right)=z^2-\frac56z+\frac16.\]

Apply the quadratic formula. The discriminant is \(25/36-4/6=1/36\), so its square root is \(1/6\). The roots are:

\[\begin{aligned}z_1&=\frac{5/6+\sqrt{(-5/6)^2-4(1/6)}}{2}=\frac12,\\[6pt]z_2&=\frac{5/6-\sqrt{(-5/6)^2-4(1/6)}}{2}=\frac13.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(|z_i|<1\), the AR lag polynomial can be inverted as a convergent series. We compute:

\[\left(1-\frac56L+\frac16L^2\right)^{-1}=\left[\left(1-\frac12L\right)\left(1-\frac13L\right)\right]^{-1}.\]

We look for \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) such that

\[\left[(1-L/2)(1-L/3)\right]^{-1}=\alpha(1-L/2)^{-1}+\beta(1-L/3)^{-1}.\]

Multiply both sides by \((1-L/2)(1-L/3)\). This removes the denominators:

\[1=\alpha(1-L/3)+\beta(1-L/2)=(\alpha+\beta)-\left(\frac\alpha3+\frac\beta2\right)L.\]

The constant must equal 1 and the coefficient of \(L\) must equal zero. Hence

\[\alpha+\beta=1,\qquad\frac\alpha3+\frac\beta2=0.\]

Multiplying the second equation by 6 gives \(2\alpha+3\beta=0\). Subtracting twice the first equation gives \(\beta=-2\), then \(\alpha=3\). Equivalently, using the inverse roots:

\[\begin{aligned}\alpha&=\frac{z_1}{z_1-z_2}=\frac{1/2}{1/2-1/3}=3,\\[6pt]\beta&=-\frac{z_2}{z_1-z_2}=-\frac{1/3}{1/2-1/3}=-2.\end{aligned}\]

Then

\[[(1-L/2)(1-L/3)]^{-1}=3(1-L/2)^{-1}-2(1-L/3)^{-1}.\]

Each term now has the form \((1-aL)^{-1}\). Since \(|a|<1\), use the geometric series \((1-aL)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a^jL^j\):

\[\begin{aligned}\Phi(L)^{-1}&=\left[\left(1-\frac12L\right)\left(1-\frac13L\right)\right]^{-1}\\&=3\left(1-\frac12L\right)^{-1}-2\left(1-\frac13L\right)^{-1}\\&=3\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac12\right)^jL^j-2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac13\right)^jL^j.\end{aligned}\]

For the MA part, apply the same inverse-root construction to \(\Theta(L)=1-L/7\): replacing \(L\) by \(z^{-1}\) and multiplying by \(z\) gives

\[z\Theta(z^{-1})=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z-\frac17=0.\]

The unique inverse root is \(z=1/7\). Since \(|z|<1\), the MA lag polynomial can also be inverted. Hence:

\[\Theta(L)^{-1}=\left(1-\frac17L\right)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17L\right)^j.\]

Conclusion

The AR inverse roots \(1/2\) and \(1/3\) satisfy \(|z_i|<1\), so \(\Phi(L)^{-1}\) exists as a convergent series, ensuring stationarity and causality.

The MA inverse root \(1/7\) also satisfies \(|z|<1\), so \(\Theta(L)^{-1}\) exists as a convergent series, ensuring fundamentalness (invertibility).

The AR and MA polynomials have no common roots, so this ARMA(2,1) representation is also minimal.

Question 3MA(∞) and AR(∞) representations

Formulas to use — Question 3

MA(∞) representation: invert the AR polynomial, then distribute the MA polynomial.

\[X_t=\mu\Phi(1)^{-1}+\Phi(L)^{-1}\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

Expand the terms for successive values of \(j\), then group the coefficients of the same shock \(\varepsilon_{t-j}\).

AR(∞) representation: invert the MA polynomial, then distribute the AR polynomial.

\[\varepsilon_t=-\mu\Theta(1)^{-1}+\Theta(L)^{-1}\Phi(L)X_t.\]

Expand the terms for successive values of \(j\), then group the coefficients of the same observation \(X_{t-j}\).

Constants: the lag operator leaves a constant unchanged, so evaluate the inverse polynomial at \(L=1\).

\[\mu_\Phi=\frac{\mu}{\Phi(1)},\qquad\mu_\Theta=-\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}.\]

Solution

We use the two inverse polynomials from Question 2 and write the representations in terms of \(X_t\).

1. MA(∞) representation

Our aim is to express \(X_t\) using current and past innovations. Start from Question 1 and multiply both sides by \(\Phi(L)^{-1}\), which exists by Question 2:

\[\begin{aligned}\Phi(L)X_t&=2+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\\X_t&=\Phi(L)^{-1}2+\Phi(L)^{-1}\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\end{aligned}\]

To keep the coefficients easy to follow, let

\[a_j=3\left(\frac12\right)^j-2\left(\frac13\right)^j,\qquad j\ge0,\qquad a_0=1.\]

A lag does not change a constant: \(L^j2=2\). Using \(\Phi(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_jL^j\), the constant term is therefore

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}2=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j=2\Phi(1)^{-1}=: \mu_\Phi=6.\]

The notation \(\Phi(1)\) means evaluating the polynomial at 1; it appears because every lag acts as the identity on a constant.

These are the coefficients found in Question 2. Since \(L^j\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-j}\), the AR inverse produces \(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\). The two lag filters commute, so we can apply \(\Theta(L)=1-L/7\) to this sum. Its \(L\) term shifts every shock back by one date:

\[\begin{aligned}X_t&=2\Phi(1)^{-1}+\left(1-\frac17L\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\\&=\mu_\Phi+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\left(\varepsilon_{t-j}-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-j-1}\right).\end{aligned}\]

Each value of \(j\) contributes two shocks, at dates \(t-j\) and \(t-j-1\). We write these contributions separately to see which dates appear more than once.

For \(j\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\):

Reindexing the second sum (algebraic method)

\[a_j=3\left(\frac12\right)^j-2\left(\frac13\right)^j.\]

Starting from

\[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\varepsilon_{t-j-1},\]

we want both sums to contain the same shock, \(\varepsilon_{t-j}\). We define the new index by

\[j_{\text{new}}=j_{\text{old}}+1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad j_{\text{old}}=j_{\text{new}}-1.\]

This changes:

  • Lower bound: \(0+1=1\).
  • Coefficient: \(a_{j_{\text{old}}}=a_{j_{\text{new}}-1}\).
  • Shock: \(\varepsilon_{t-j_{\text{old}}-1}=\varepsilon_{t-(j_{\text{new}}-1)-1}=\varepsilon_{t-j_{\text{new}}}\).

Renaming the new index \(j\), we obtain

\[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\varepsilon_{t-j-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j-1}\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Reindexing does not change the sum; it only allows us to group the coefficients of the same shock.

Substituting into the original expression gives

\[X_t=\mu_\Phi+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\varepsilon_{t-j}-\frac17\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j-1}\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

We isolate the \(j=0\) term of the first sum. Both remaining sums start at \(j=1\), so their coefficients can be combined:

\[\boxed{X_t=\mu_\Phi+a_0\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(a_j-\frac17a_{j-1}\right)\varepsilon_{t-j}.}\]

The first coefficient is \(a_0=1\). For \(j\ge1\), the coefficient of \(\varepsilon_{t-j}\) is \(a_j-a_{j-1}/7\). Therefore,

\[\psi_0=1,\qquad\psi_j=a_j-\frac17a_{j-1},\quad j\ge1.\]

We have

\[a_j=3\left(\frac12\right)^j-2\left(\frac13\right)^j.\]

Therefore,

\[a_{j-1}=3\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}-2\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}.\]

Calculating \(a_j-\frac17a_{j-1}\)

Substituting both expressions gives

\[\begin{aligned}a_j-\frac17a_{j-1}={}&3\left(\frac12\right)^j-2\left(\frac13\right)^j\\&-\frac17\left[3\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}-2\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}\right].\end{aligned}\]

Factoring out the common powers,

\[\begin{aligned}a_j-\frac17a_{j-1}={}&3\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}\left(\frac12-\frac17\right)\\&-2\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}\left(\frac13-\frac17\right).\end{aligned}\]

Simplifying,

\[\boxed{a_j-\frac17a_{j-1}=\frac{15}{14}\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}-\frac8{21}\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}.}\]
\[\begin{aligned}j=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad&\varepsilon_t\quad{\color{#fb7185}\boxed{-\frac17\varepsilon_{t-1}}}\\[12pt]j=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad&{\color{#fb7185}\boxed{a_1\varepsilon_{t-1}}}\quad{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{-\frac17a_1\varepsilon_{t-2}}}\\[12pt]j=2\quad\Rightarrow\quad&{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{a_2\varepsilon_{t-2}}}\quad{\color{#4ade80}\boxed{-\frac17a_2\varepsilon_{t-3}}}\\&\vdots\end{aligned}\]

Let’s regroup the terms by factoring out. The same colour identifies terms multiplying the same shock:

\[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_t&+{\color{#fb7185}\boxed{\left\{a_1-\frac17\right\}\varepsilon_{t-1}}}\\&+{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{\left\{a_2-\frac17a_1\right\}\varepsilon_{t-2}}}+\cdots.\end{aligned}\]

For a given shock \(\varepsilon_{t-j}\) with \(j\ge1\), the first part of the sum contributes \(a_j\); the second part contributes \(-a_{j-1}/7\). Their combined coefficient is therefore \(a_j-a_{j-1}/7\). The current shock has coefficient \(a_0=1\):

\[X_t=\mu_\Phi+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(a_j-\frac17a_{j-1}\right)\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Now substitute \(a_j\) and

\[a_{j-1}=3\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}-2\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}:\]
\[X_t=\mu_\Phi+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\{\left[3\left(\frac12\right)^{j}-2\left(\frac13\right)^{j}\right]-\frac17\left[3\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}-2\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}\right]\right\}\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Factor out \((1/2)^{j-1}\) and \((1/3)^{j-1}\), using \(a^j=a\,a^{j-1}\). This turns each difference of two powers into a common power times a difference:

\[=\mu_\Phi+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left[3\left(\frac12-\frac17\right)\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}-2\left(\frac13-\frac17\right)\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}\right]\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

where

\[\mu_\Phi=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left[3\left(\frac12\right)^{j}-2\left(\frac13\right)^{j}\right]=6,\qquad\Phi(1)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-5/6+1/6}=3.\]
\[X_t=6+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\underbrace{\left[\frac{15}{14}\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}-\frac8{21}\left(\frac13\right)^{j-1}\right]}_{\psi_j}\varepsilon_{t-j},\qquad\psi_0=1.\]

2. AR(∞) representation

Our aim is now to recover \(\varepsilon_t\) from current and past observations. Start again from the original-variable equation and move the intercept to the other side:

\[\Phi(L)X_t=2+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t=\Phi(L)X_t-2.\]

Multiply both terms on the right by \(\Theta(L)^{-1}\). On the left, \(\Theta(L)^{-1}\Theta(L)=1\), so only \(\varepsilon_t\) remains:

\[\varepsilon_t=\Theta(L)^{-1}\Phi(L)X_t-\Theta(L)^{-1}2.\]

From Question 2, \(\Theta(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1/7)^jL^j\). For the constant, every lag still gives 2, so

\[-\Theta(L)^{-1}2=-2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^j=-\frac{2}{1-1/7}=-\frac73=: \mu_\Theta.\]

This is the source of the term \(-2\Theta(1)^{-1}\). The minus sign comes from moving 2 to the right before applying the inverse.

For the observations, \(\Phi(L)\) and \(\Theta(L)^{-1}\) can be applied in either order because both are filters in the same lag operator. Use \(L^jX_t=X_{t-j}\):

\[\Theta(L)^{-1}\Phi(L)X_t=\Phi(L)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j}.\]

Finally, distribute \(\Phi(L)=1-5L/6+L^2/6\) over the sum. On each observation, it gives

\[\Phi(L)X_{t-j}=X_{t-j}-\frac56X_{t-j-1}+\frac16X_{t-j-2}.\]

Putting the constant and the observation terms together gives the two equivalent lines:

\[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_t&=-2\Theta(1)^{-1}+\left(1-\frac56L+\frac16L^2\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j}\\&=\mu_\Theta+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^j\left(X_{t-j}-\frac56X_{t-j-1}+\frac16X_{t-j-2}\right).\end{aligned}\]

For \(j\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\):

Reindexing the shifted sums (algebraic method)

First distribute the brackets into three sums. The constant \(\mu_\Theta\) stays outside:

\[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_t={}&\mu_\Theta+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j}\\&-\frac56\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j-1}\\&+\frac16\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j-2}.\end{aligned}\]

To combine the three sums, we want each remaining sum to start at \(j=1\) and to share the weight \((1/7)^{j-1}\). The bracket will contain both \(X_{t-j}\) and \(X_{t-j-1}\).

First sum — isolate the current observation:

Its \(j=0\) term is \(X_t\). Remove this term from the sum, then write \((1/7)^j=(1/7)(1/7)^{j-1}\):

\[\begin{aligned}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j}&=X_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j}\\&=X_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-1}\frac17X_{t-j}.\end{aligned}\]

Second sum — shift the index by one:

\[j_{\mathrm{new}}=j_{\mathrm{old}}+1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad j_{\mathrm{old}}=j_{\mathrm{new}}-1.\]
  • Lower bound: \(0+1=1\).
  • Coefficient: \((1/7)^{j_{\mathrm{old}}}=(1/7)^{j_{\mathrm{new}}-1}\).
  • Observation: \(X_{t-j_{\mathrm{old}}-1}=X_{t-(j_{\mathrm{new}}-1)-1}=X_{t-j_{\mathrm{new}}}\).

Rename the new index \(j\). The multiplier \(-5/6\) is unchanged:

\[-\frac56\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j-1}=-\frac56\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-1}X_{t-j}.\]

Third sum — also shift the index by one:

Use the same change of index so that this sum also begins at 1. Its observation keeps one extra lag:

\[j_{\mathrm{new}}=j_{\mathrm{old}}+1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad j_{\mathrm{old}}=j_{\mathrm{new}}-1.\]
  • Lower bound: \(0+1=1\).
  • Coefficient: \((1/7)^{j_{\mathrm{old}}}=(1/7)^{j_{\mathrm{new}}-1}\).
  • Observation: \(X_{t-j_{\mathrm{old}}-2}=X_{t-(j_{\mathrm{new}}-1)-2}=X_{t-j_{\mathrm{new}}-1}\).

Rename the new index \(j\). The multiplier \(1/6\) is unchanged:

\[\frac16\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^jX_{t-j-2}=\frac16\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-1}X_{t-j-1}.\]

Reindexing only relabels the terms; it does not change their dates or their coefficients. In particular, the third sum now contains \(X_{t-j-1}\).

Combine the three sums: they now have the same lower bound and the same geometric weight.

\[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_t={}&\mu_\Theta+X_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-1}\frac17X_{t-j}\\&-\frac56\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-1}X_{t-j}\\&+\frac16\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-1}X_{t-j-1}.\end{aligned}\]

Bring the terms inside one sum. The first two terms multiply \(X_{t-j}\), so their coefficients add to \(1/7-5/6\). The third term remains \((1/6)X_{t-j-1}\):

\[\varepsilon_t=\mu_\Theta+X_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-1}\left[\left(\frac17-\frac56\right)X_{t-j}+\frac16X_{t-j-1}\right].\]
\[\begin{aligned}j=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad&X_t\quad{\color{#fb7185}\boxed{-\frac56X_{t-1}}}\quad{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{+\frac16X_{t-2}}}\\[12pt]j=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad&{\color{#fb7185}\boxed{\left(\frac17\right)^{1}X_{t-1}}}\quad{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{-\frac56\left(\frac17\right)^{1}X_{t-2}}}\quad{\color{#4ade80}\boxed{+\frac16\left(\frac17\right)^{1}X_{t-3}}}\\[12pt]j=2\quad\Rightarrow\quad&{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{\left(\frac17\right)^{2}X_{t-2}}}\quad{\color{#4ade80}\boxed{-\frac56\left(\frac17\right)^{2}X_{t-3}}}\quad{\color{#f0abfc}\boxed{+\frac16\left(\frac17\right)^{2}X_{t-4}}}\\&\vdots\end{aligned}\]

Let’s regroup the terms by factoring out. The same colour identifies terms multiplying the same observation:

\[X_t+{\color{#fb7185}\boxed{\left[\left(\frac17\right)^1-\frac56\right]X_{t-1}}}+{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{\left[\left(\frac17\right)^2-\frac56\left(\frac17\right)^1+\frac16\right]X_{t-2}}}+\cdots.\]

To write the remaining terms as one sum starting at \(j=1\), isolate \(X_t\), then shift the second and third original sums by one index. Their common weight is \((1/7)^{j-1}\). The first two contributions combine into \((1/7-5/6)X_{t-j}\), while the third remains \((1/6)X_{t-j-1}\). The button above shows each change of index. This gives:

\[\varepsilon_t=\mu_\Theta+X_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^{j-1}\left[\left(\frac17-\frac56\right)X_{t-j}+\frac16X_{t-j-1}\right].\]

where

\[\mu_\Theta=-2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac17\right)^j=-\frac73,\qquad\Theta(1)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-1/7}=\frac76.\]

Question 4Autocorrelation function

Formulas to use — Question 4

Yule–Walker equations for ARMA(2,1):

\[\begin{aligned}\gamma(0)&=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(2)\\&\quad+\sigma_\varepsilon^2[1+\theta_1(\phi_1+\theta_1)],\end{aligned}\]\[(1-\phi_2)\gamma(1)=\phi_1\gamma(0)+\theta_1\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\]\[\gamma(2)=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(0).\]

Insert the AR and MA coefficients, then solve for \(\gamma(0),\gamma(1),\gamma(2)\), the autocovariances at lags 0, 1 and 2.

Higher lags:

\[\gamma(h)=\phi_1\gamma(h-1)+\phi_2\gamma(h-2),\qquad h\ge2.\]

Apply the recurrence using the initial autocovariances.

Autocorrelation:

\[\rho(h)=\frac{\gamma(h)}{\gamma(0)}.\]

Conclusion: divide each autocovariance by the variance \(\gamma(0)\) to obtain the ACF.

Solution

To compute the autocorrelation function, let’s use the Yule–Walker equations. For a generic mean-adjusted ARMA(2,1) process:

\[\widetilde X_t=\phi_1\widetilde X_{t-1}+\phi_2\widetilde X_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}.\]

Multiply by \(\widetilde X_{t-h}\) and take expectations to get the autocovariance \(\gamma(h)\). Here \(\phi_1=5/6\), \(\phi_2=-1/6\), \(\theta_1=-1/7\), and \(\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)\).

Because the process is centred, \(\gamma(h)=E[\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t-h}]\). Stationarity means that the distance between the dates determines the covariance. Causality also tells us that an innovation is uncorrelated with earlier observations:

\[E[\varepsilon_s\widetilde X_u]=0\quad(u<s),\qquad E[\varepsilon_s\widetilde X_s]=\sigma^2.\]

The second identity holds because \(\widetilde X_s\) contains \(\varepsilon_s\) with coefficient 1, and its other terms involve earlier innovations.

Lag 0

\[\begin{aligned}E[\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t}]={}&\phi_1E[\widetilde X_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t}]+\phi_2E[\widetilde X_{t-2}\widetilde X_{t}]\\&+E[\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_{t}]+\theta_1E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t}].\end{aligned}\]

The contemporaneous shock contributes \(E[\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_t]=\sigma^2\). For the lagged shock, substitute the model for \(\widetilde X_t\): only \(\phi_1\widetilde X_{t-1}\) and \(\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}\) contribute, giving \((\phi_1+\theta_1)\sigma^2\).

\[\begin{aligned}\gamma(0)&=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(2)+\sigma^2+\theta_1E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\widetilde X_t]\\&=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(2)+\sigma^2\\&\quad+\theta_1E[(\phi_1\widetilde X_{t-1}+\phi_2\widetilde X_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1})\varepsilon_{t-1}]\\&=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(2)+\sigma^2+\theta_1(\phi_1+\theta_1)\sigma^2\\&=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(2)+\sigma^2[1+\theta_1(\phi_1+\theta_1)].\end{aligned}\]

Lag 1

Now \(\varepsilon_t\) is uncorrelated with \(\widetilde X_{t-1}\), while \(E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t-1}]=\sigma^2\). Move \(\phi_2\gamma(1)\) to the left and divide by \(1-\phi_2\) to isolate \(\gamma(1)\).

\[\begin{aligned}E[\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t-1}]={}&\phi_1E[\widetilde X_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t-1}]+\phi_2E[\widetilde X_{t-2}\widetilde X_{t-1}]\\&+E[\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_{t-1}]+\theta_1E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t-1}].\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\gamma(1)&=\phi_1\gamma(0)+\phi_2\gamma(1)+\theta_1\sigma^2\\&=\frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2}\gamma(0)+\frac{\theta_1}{1-\phi_2}\sigma^2.\end{aligned}\]

Lag 2

Both innovations in the model occur after date \(t-2\), so their covariances with \(\widetilde X_{t-2}\) are zero. Then substitute the expression for \(\gamma(1)\) from the previous step and collect the terms multiplying \(\gamma(0)\).

\[\begin{aligned}E[\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t-2}]={}&\phi_1E[\widetilde X_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t-2}]+\phi_2E[\widetilde X_{t-2}\widetilde X_{t-2}]\\&+E[\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_{t-2}]+\theta_1E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t-2}].\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\gamma(2)&=\phi_1\gamma(1)+\phi_2\gamma(0)\\&=\frac{\phi_1^2}{1-\phi_2}\gamma(0)+\frac{\phi_1\theta_1}{1-\phi_2}\sigma^2+\phi_2\gamma(0)\\&=\frac{\phi_1^2+\phi_2-\phi_2^2}{1-\phi_2}\gamma(0)+\frac{\phi_1\theta_1}{1-\phi_2}\sigma^2.\end{aligned}\]

Lag 3

The same reasoning removes both innovation terms. The observations on the right are two and one periods away from \(t-3\), which gives \(\gamma(2)\) and \(\gamma(1)\).

\[\begin{aligned}E[\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t-3}]={}&\phi_1E[\widetilde X_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t-3}]+\phi_2E[\widetilde X_{t-2}\widetilde X_{t-3}]\\&+E[\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_{t-3}]+\theta_1E[\varepsilon_{t-1}\widetilde X_{t-3}].\end{aligned}\]
\[\gamma(3)=\phi_1\gamma(2)+\phi_2\gamma(1).\]

More generally, for \(h\ge2\):

\[\gamma(h)=\phi_1\gamma(h-1)+\phi_2\gamma(h-2).\]

Autocovariances

Substitute the expressions for \(\gamma(1)\) and \(\gamma(2)\) into the lag-0 equation. Multiply by \(1-\phi_2\) to remove their denominators, then collect every term containing \(\gamma(0)\) on the left:

\[\begin{aligned}&\left[(1-\phi_2)-\phi_1^2-\phi_2(\phi_1^2+\phi_2-\phi_2^2)\right]\gamma(0)\\&\quad=\left[\phi_1\theta_1+\phi_2\phi_1\theta_1+(1-\phi_2)\{1+\theta_1(\phi_1+\theta_1)\}\right]\sigma^2.\end{aligned}\]

Expand the brackets and divide by the coefficient of \(\gamma(0)\). Finally insert \(\phi_1=5/6\), \(\phi_2=-1/6\), and \(\theta_1=-1/7\):

\[\begin{aligned}\gamma(0)&=\frac{(1-\phi_2)+\theta_1[2\phi_1+\theta_1-\phi_2\theta_1]}{(1-\phi_2)-\phi_1^2(1+\phi_2)-\phi_2^2+\phi_2^3}\sigma^2\\&=\frac{12}{7}\sigma^2\approx1.7143\sigma^2.\end{aligned}\]

We now know the variance. Put \(\gamma(0)=(12/7)\sigma^2\) into the lag-1 and lag-2 expressions, then use \(\gamma(3)=\phi_1\gamma(2)+\phi_2\gamma(1)\). This gives the remaining autocovariances:

\[\begin{aligned}\gamma(1)&=\frac{(12/7)\phi_1+\theta_1}{1-\phi_2}\sigma^2=\frac{54}{49}\sigma^2\approx1.1020\sigma^2,\\[6pt]\gamma(2)&=\frac{(12/7)(\phi_1^2+\phi_2-\phi_2^2)+\phi_1\theta_1}{1-\phi_2}\sigma^2=\frac{31}{49}\sigma^2\approx0.6327\sigma^2,\\[6pt]\gamma(3)&=\frac{(12/7)\phi_1(\phi_1^2+2\phi_2-\phi_2^2)+\theta_1(\phi_1^2+\phi_2)}{1-\phi_2}\sigma^2\\&=\frac{101}{294}\sigma^2\approx0.3435\sigma^2.\end{aligned}\]

Autocorrelation function

The ACF is \(\rho(h)=\gamma(h)/\gamma(0)\). Therefore:

\[\begin{aligned}\rho(0)&=1,\\[4pt]\rho(1)&=\frac{(54/49)\sigma^2}{(12/7)\sigma^2}=\frac9{14}\approx0.6429,\\[4pt]\rho(2)&=\frac{(31/49)\sigma^2}{(12/7)\sigma^2}=\frac{31}{84}\approx0.3690,\\[4pt]\rho(3)&=\frac{(101/294)\sigma^2}{(12/7)\sigma^2}=\frac{101}{504}\approx0.2004.\end{aligned}\]
\[\rho(h)=\frac56\rho(h-1)-\frac16\rho(h-2)\quad(h\ge2),\qquad\rho(-h)=\rho(h).\]

Question 5First three terms of the PACF

Formulas to use — Question 5

Correlation matrix:

\[(R_h)_{ij}=\rho(|i-j|),\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,h,\]\[\boldsymbol\rho_h=(\rho(1),\ldots,\rho(h))^{\mathsf T}.\]

Use the ACF from Question 4 to build \(R_h\) for each lag \(h=1,2,3\).

Cramer’s rule for the last projection coefficient:

\[\alpha(h)=\frac{\det(R_h^\star)}{\det(R_h)}.\]

Replace the last column of \(R_h\) by \(\boldsymbol\rho_h\) to obtain \(R_h^\star\). Calculate both determinants, then divide.

Conclusion: report \(\alpha(1),\alpha(2),\alpha(3)\), the first three PACF terms.

Solution

The PACF is obtained via Cramer’s rule: \(r_{kk}=\det(R_{kk}^{\star})/\det(R_{kk})\). Replace the last column of the correlation matrix by \((\rho(1),\ldots,\rho(k))^{\mathsf T}\).

Here \(r_{kk}\) is the same PACF value denoted by \(\alpha(k)\) in the formula box. It is the coefficient on \(\widetilde X_{t-k}\) when projecting \(\widetilde X_t\) on its first \(k\) lags. The projection equations are

\[R_{kk}\begin{pmatrix}r_{k1}\\\vdots\\r_{kk}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho(1)\\\vdots\\\rho(k)\end{pmatrix},\qquad (R_{kk})_{ij}=\rho(|i-j|).\]

The diagonal entries are \(\rho(0)=1\). Cramer’s rule isolates the last coefficient by replacing the last column with the right-hand-side vector.

First term

With a single lag, the equation is \(1\cdot r_{11}=\rho(1)\).

\[r_{11}=\rho(1)=\frac9{14}\approx0.6429.\]

Second term

For two lags, replace the second column by \((\rho(1),\rho(2))^{\mathsf T}\). Each 2×2 determinant is the product of the diagonal entries minus the product of the off-diagonal entries.

\[\begin{aligned}r_{22}&=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}1&\rho(1)\\\rho(1)&\rho(2)\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}1&\rho(1)\\\rho(1)&1\end{vmatrix}}\\&=\frac{\rho(2)-\rho(1)^2}{1-\rho(1)^2}\\&=\frac{31/84-(9/14)^2}{1-(9/14)^2}=-\frac{26}{345}\approx-0.0754.\end{aligned}\]

Third term

For three lags, replace the third column by \((\rho(1),\rho(2),\rho(3))^{\mathsf T}\). The first two columns stay unchanged.

\[r_{33}=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}1&\rho(1)&\rho(1)\\\rho(1)&1&\rho(2)\\\rho(2)&\rho(1)&\rho(3)\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}1&\rho(1)&\rho(2)\\\rho(1)&1&\rho(1)\\\rho(2)&\rho(1)&1\end{vmatrix}}.\]

Expand each determinant along its first row, with alternating signs \(+, -, +\). To keep this step readable, write \(u=\rho(1)\), \(v=\rho(2)\), and \(w=\rho(3)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\det R_{33}&=(1-u^2)-u(u-uv)+v(u^2-v)\\&=1-2u^2(1-v)-v^2,\\\det R_{33}^{\star}&=(w-uv)-u(uw-v^2)+u(u^2-v)\\&=w-u(2v+uw-v^2-u^2).\end{aligned}\]

Replace \(u,v,w\) by the correlations and divide the two determinants:

\[r_{33}=\frac{\rho(3)-\rho(1)[2\rho(2)+\rho(1)\rho(3)-\rho(2)^2-\rho(1)^2]}{1-2\rho(1)^2[1-\rho(2)]-\rho(2)^2}.\]

Substitute \(\rho(1)=9/14\), \(\rho(2)=31/84\), and \(\rho(3)=101/504\):

\[r_{33}=\frac{-13/3528}{16907/49392}=-\frac{182}{16907}\approx-0.0108.\]
\[r_{11}\approx0.6429,\qquad r_{22}\approx-0.0754,\qquad r_{33}\approx-0.0108.\]

Question 6Best linear forecast for all horizons

Formulas to use — Question 6

Best linear forecast from the MA(∞) representation:

\[\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=m_X+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}.\]

Insert the mean and MA weights found earlier. Keep shocks dated at or before \(t\); replace future shocks by zero.

Recursive forecasts from the original equation:

\[\begin{aligned}\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}&=c+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i X^*_{t+h-i\mid t}\\&\quad+\sum_{k=h}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{t+h-k},\end{aligned}\]\[X^*_{t+s\mid t}=\begin{cases}X_{t+s},&s\le0,\\\widehat X_{t+s\mid t},&s>0.\end{cases}\]

Keep the intercept \(c\) from the original equation. Use observed values for past dates and previously calculated forecasts for future dates. An empty MA sum is zero.

Conclusion: calculate horizons 1, 2 and 3, write the general forecast, then take its limit as \(h\to\infty\).

Solution

The forecast is formed at date \(t\). Current and past innovations are recoverable by fundamentalness, and future innovations have zero best linear forecast.

1. One-step forecast

At date \(t+1\), the model is

\[X_{t+1}=2+\frac56X_t-\frac16X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t+1}-\frac17\varepsilon_t.\]

Only \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) is unknown at date \(t\). Its best linear forecast is zero, so

\[\boxed{\widehat X_{t+1\mid t}=2+\frac56X_t-\frac16X_{t-1}-\frac17\varepsilon_t.}\]

2. Two-step forecast

At date \(t+2\), both \(\varepsilon_{t+2}\) and \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) have zero forecast. We replace \(X_{t+1}\) by its one-step forecast:

\[\widehat X_{t+2\mid t}=2+\frac56\widehat X_{t+1\mid t}-\frac16X_t.\]

Substituting the previous result gives

\[\widehat X_{t+2\mid t}=2+\frac56\left(2+\frac56X_t-\frac16X_{t-1}-\frac17\varepsilon_t\right)-\frac16X_t.\]
\[\boxed{\widehat X_{t+2\mid t}=\frac{11}{3}+\frac{19}{36}X_t-\frac5{36}X_{t-1}-\frac5{42}\varepsilon_t.}\]

3. Three-step forecast

Applying the same recursion,

\[\widehat X_{t+3\mid t}=2+\frac56\widehat X_{t+2\mid t}-\frac16\widehat X_{t+1\mid t}.\]

The coefficients after substitution are

\[\begin{aligned}2+\frac56\cdot\frac{11}{3}-\frac16\cdot2&=\frac{85}{18},\\\frac56\cdot\frac{19}{36}-\frac16\cdot\frac56&=\frac{65}{216},\\-\frac56\cdot\frac5{36}+\frac16\cdot\frac16&=-\frac{19}{216},\\-\frac56\cdot\frac5{42}+\frac16\cdot\frac17&=-\frac{19}{252}.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\widehat X_{t+3\mid t}=\frac{85}{18}+\frac{65}{216}X_t-\frac{19}{216}X_{t-1}-\frac{19}{252}\varepsilon_t.}\]

4. General horizon

For every \(h\ge2\), the forecast satisfies

\[\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=2+\frac56\widehat X_{t+h-1\mid t}-\frac16\widehat X_{t+h-2\mid t},\]\[\widehat X_{t\mid t}=X_t.\]

To solve the recurrence, let \(d_h=\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}-6\). Then \(d_h=(5/6)d_{h-1}-(1/6)d_{h-2}\), with

\[d_0=X_t-6,\]\[d_1=\frac56(X_t-6)-\frac16(X_{t-1}-6)-\frac17\varepsilon_t.\]

To solve this second-order recurrence, try \(d_h=r^h\). Substitution gives \(r^2-(5/6)r+1/6=0\), the same characteristic equation as in Question 2. Its roots are \(1/2\) and \(1/3\), so \(d_h=C(1/2)^h+D(1/3)^h\).

Set \(h=0\) and \(h=1\) to match the two initial forecasts: \(C+D=d_0\) and \(C/2+D/3=d_1\). Multiply the second equation by 6, then subtract twice the first to get \(C=6d_1-2d_0\). Finally use \(D=d_0-C\):

\[C=6d_1-2d_0,\qquad D=3d_0-6d_1.\]

Hence the forecast at any horizon is

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=6&+(6d_1-2d_0)\left(\frac12\right)^h\\&+(3d_0-6d_1)\left(\frac13\right)^h.\end{aligned}}\]

The same forecast follows from the MA representation. A shock \(\varepsilon_{t+h-j}\) is known at date \(t\) exactly when \(t+h-j\le t\), or \(j\ge h\). This explains the lower bound of the forecast sum:

\[\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=6+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}.\]

5. Infinite horizon

Both geometric terms tend to zero. Therefore,

\[\boxed{\lim_{h\to\infty}\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=6.}\]

Question 7Forecast errors and their variances

Formulas to use — Question 7

Forecast error:

\[e_t(h)=X_{t+h}-\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}=\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}.\]

Subtract the forecast from the MA representation. Keep only the future shocks.

Forecast-error variance:

\[\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2.\]

Use the weights from Question 3. Square the first \(h\) weights, add them, then multiply by the innovation variance.

Geometric sums:

\[\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}a^j=\frac{1-a^h}{1-a},\qquad a\ne1.\]

Expand \(\psi_j^2\), then sum each geometric term. For the infinite horizon, take the limit of the variance as \(h\to\infty\).

Conclusion: report the error and its variance for each finite horizon, then the limiting error variance.

Solution

We subtract the forecast in Question 6 from the future observation, using the MA coefficients found in Question 3.

1. Forecast error

\[\begin{aligned}e_t(h)&=X_{t+h}-\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}\\&=\left(6+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}\right)-\left(6+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}\right).\end{aligned}\]

The known terms cancel, leaving only future shocks:

\[\boxed{e_t(h)=\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}.}\]

For a given horizon \(h\), the remaining indices run from 0 to \(h-1\). The weights come from Question 3: \(\psi_0=1\), \(\psi_1=15/14-8/21=29/42\), and \(\psi_2=15/28-8/63=103/252\). Substituting these weights gives

\[\begin{aligned}e_t(1)&=\varepsilon_{t+1},\\e_t(2)&=\varepsilon_{t+2}+\frac{29}{42}\varepsilon_{t+1},\\e_t(3)&=\varepsilon_{t+3}+\frac{29}{42}\varepsilon_{t+2}+\frac{103}{252}\varepsilon_{t+1}.\end{aligned}\]

2. Error variances

The variance of a weighted sum equals the sum of the squared weights times the individual variances, plus cross-covariance terms. Here innovations at different dates are uncorrelated, so

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]={}&\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\\&+2\sum_{0\le i<j\le h-1}\psi_i\psi_j\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_{t+h-i},\varepsilon_{t+h-j})}_{0}.\end{aligned}\]

Only the squared-weight terms remain:

\[\boxed{V_h:=\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2.}\]

For the first three horizons,

\[\begin{aligned}V_1&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\\V_2&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\left[1+\left(\frac{29}{42}\right)^2\right]=\frac{2605}{1764}\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\\V_3&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\left[1+\left(\frac{29}{42}\right)^2+\left(\frac{103}{252}\right)^2\right]=\frac{104389}{63504}\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\end{aligned}\]

3. Variance at a general horizon

For \(j\ge1\), we have \(\psi_j=(15/14)(1/2)^{j-1}-(8/21)(1/3)^{j-1}\). Squaring and setting \(n=j-1\),

\[\psi_j^2=\frac{225}{196}\left(\frac14\right)^n+\frac{64}{441}\left(\frac19\right)^n-\frac{40}{49}\left(\frac16\right)^n.\]

The term \(\psi_0^2=1\) is kept separately. For the other terms, \(j=1,\ldots,h-1\) becomes \(n=0,\ldots,h-2\). The identity \((A-B)^2=A^2+B^2-2AB\) explains the two positive terms and the negative cross term above. Apply \(\sum_{n=0}^{h-2}a^n=(1-a^{h-1})/(1-a)\) to each geometric sum:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{V_h}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}&=1+\frac{225}{196}\frac{1-(1/4)^{h-1}}{1-1/4}\\&\quad+\frac{64}{441}\frac{1-(1/9)^{h-1}}{1-1/9}-\frac{40}{49}\frac{1-(1/6)^{h-1}}{1-1/6}\\&=1+\frac{75}{49}[1-(1/4)^{h-1}]\\&\quad+\frac8{49}[1-(1/9)^{h-1}]-\frac{48}{49}[1-(1/6)^{h-1}].\end{aligned}\]

Since \(1+75/49+8/49-48/49=12/7\), this simplifies to

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}V_h=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\Bigg[\frac{12}{7}&-\frac{75}{49}\left(\frac14\right)^{h-1}\\&-\frac8{49}\left(\frac19\right)^{h-1}+\frac{48}{49}\left(\frac16\right)^{h-1}\Bigg],\qquad h\ge1.\end{aligned}}\]

For \(h=1\), the geometric sums are empty and the formula gives \(V_1=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\).

4. Infinite horizon

The forecast tends to 6, so the long-horizon forecast error is approximately \(X_{t+h}-6\). In the variance formula, each geometric power tends to zero. Therefore,

\[\boxed{\lim_{h\to\infty}V_h=\frac{12}{7}\sigma_\varepsilon^2=\gamma(0).}\]

Moreover, \(V_{h+1}-V_h=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\psi_h^2\ge0\), so the error variance increases towards this limit.

Question 8Prediction interval

Formulas to use — Question 8

Prediction interval under iid Gaussian innovations:

\[PI_{1-\alpha}(h)=\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[e_t(h)]}.\]

Use the forecast from Question 6 and the error variance from Question 7. Choose the standard-normal quantile \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) for coverage \(1-\alpha\).

For 95% coverage:

\[z_{0.975}\simeq1.96.\]

Subtract the margin for the lower endpoint; add it for the upper endpoint.

Conclusion: give both endpoints for the requested horizons, stating the Gaussian assumption.

Solution

For a normal prediction interval, we additionally assume \(\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2)\). We treat the model parameters as known and use \(V_h\) from Question 7.

1. General prediction interval

The error in Question 7 contains only future innovations. Under the iid Gaussian assumption, they are independent of the information available at date \(t\); their weighted sum is Gaussian with mean zero and variance \(V_h\). Divide by its standard deviation \(\sqrt{V_h}\) to obtain a standard normal variable:

\[e_t(h)=X_{t+h}-\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}\sim N(0,V_h),\qquad\frac{e_t(h)}{\sqrt{V_h}}\sim N(0,1).\]

If \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) is the standard-normal quantile, then

\[P\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2}\le\frac{X_{t+h}-\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}}{\sqrt{V_h}}\le z_{1-\alpha/2}\right)=1-\alpha.\]

Multiplying by \(\sqrt{V_h}\) and adding the forecast gives the interval

\[\boxed{PI_{1-\alpha}(h)=\left[\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{V_h},\;\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{V_h}\right].}\]

2. Interval at 95%

For 95% coverage, \(\alpha=0.05\). We leave \(\alpha/2=0.025\) in each tail of the normal distribution, so the upper quantile is \(z_{0.975}\approx1.96\). Since \(V_h=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2\), its square root is \(\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2}\). Therefore,

\[PI_{95\%}(h)=\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}\pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2}.\]

Using the forecasts in Question 6 and the variances in Question 7, the first three intervals are

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}PI_{95\%}(1)&=\widehat X_{t+1\mid t}\pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon,\\PI_{95\%}(2)&=\widehat X_{t+2\mid t}\pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{2605}{1764}},\\PI_{95\%}(3)&=\widehat X_{t+3\mid t}\pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{104389}{63504}}.\end{aligned}}\]

In each expression, the minus sign gives the lower endpoint and the plus sign gives the upper endpoint.

3. Infinite horizon

Since \(\widehat X_{t+h\mid t}\to6\) and \(V_h\to(12/7)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), the limiting interval is

\[\boxed{PI_{95\%}(\infty)=6\pm1.96\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{12}{7}}.}\]

These normal intervals require the additional Gaussian assumption; weak white noise alone does not determine the distribution needed for normal coverage.
