PROBLEM SET 4

Exercise 1

ARMA(1,1) process: forecasts and IRFs

Consider the following ARMA(1,1) process:

\[X_t=\frac12X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t-\frac13\varepsilon_{t-1},\]

where \(\varepsilon_t\) is a zero-mean weak white noise with variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2>0\).

  1. Give the MA(\(\infty\)) representation of \(X_t\).
  2. Determine the best linear forecast for \(h=1,2,3,\ldots,\infty\).
  3. Determine the forecast errors and their variance for \(h=1,2,3,\ldots,\infty\).
  4. Determine the prediction interval.
  5. Compute the IRF for \(h=1,2,3,\ldots,\infty\), and the long-run cumulative IRF.

Question 1MA(∞) representation

Formulas to use — Question 1

Invert the AR polynomial: express the process as a filter of innovations.

\[X_t=\Phi(L)^{-1}\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\qquad (1-aL)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a^jL^j,\quad |a|<1.\]

Collect the same shock: after distributing the MA polynomial, combine the coefficients multiplying each \(\varepsilon_{t-j}\). These are the MA weights \(\psi_j\).

Solution

Write the lag-polynomial equation:

\[\underbrace{\left(1-\frac12L\right)}_{\Phi(L)}X_t=\underbrace{\left(1-\frac13L\right)}_{\Theta(L)}\varepsilon_t.\]

Since \(|1/2|<1\), expand the AR inverse as a geometric series. Let \(a_j=(1/2)^j\), so \(a_0=1\):

\[\begin{aligned}X_t&=\frac{\Theta(L)}{\Phi(L)}\varepsilon_t\\&=\left(1-\frac13L\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\\&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\left(\varepsilon_{t-j}-\frac13\varepsilon_{t-j-1}\right).\end{aligned}\]

The lag operator shifts each innovation back one period. For \(j\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\):

Reindexing the second sum

Set \(j_{\mathrm{new}}=j_{\mathrm{old}}+1\). The lower bound becomes 1, the coefficient becomes \(a_{j-1}\), and the shock becomes \(\varepsilon_{t-j}\):

\[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\varepsilon_{t-j-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j-1}\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Isolate \(j=0\) in the first sum, then combine:

\[X_t=a_0\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(a_j-\frac13a_{j-1}\right)\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Reindexing leaves the sum unchanged; it puts the same innovation date in both sums.

\[\begin{aligned}j=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad&\varepsilon_t\quad{\color{#fb7185}\boxed{-\frac13\varepsilon_{t-1}}}\\[10pt]j=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad&{\color{#fb7185}\boxed{a_1\varepsilon_{t-1}}}\quad{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{-\frac13a_1\varepsilon_{t-2}}}\\[10pt]j=2\quad\Rightarrow\quad&{\color{#60a5fa}\boxed{a_2\varepsilon_{t-2}}}\quad{\color{#4ade80}\boxed{-\frac13a_2\varepsilon_{t-3}}}\\&\vdots\end{aligned}\]

Terms with the same colour multiply the same shock. Collecting them gives

\[\begin{aligned}X_t=\varepsilon_t&+{\color{#fb7185}\left(a_1-\frac13a_0\right)\varepsilon_{t-1}}\\&+{\color{#60a5fa}\left(a_2-\frac13a_1\right)\varepsilon_{t-2}}+\cdots.\end{aligned}\]

For \(j\ge1\), substitute \(a_j\) and factor out the common power:

\[\begin{aligned}a_j-\frac13a_{j-1}&=\left(\frac12\right)^j-\frac13\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}\\&=\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}\\&=\frac16\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{X_t=\varepsilon_t+\frac16\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1}\varepsilon_{t-j}.}\]
\[\psi_0=1,\qquad\psi_j=\frac16\left(\frac12\right)^{j-1},\quad j\ge1.\]

Question 2Best linear forecasts

Formulas to use — Question 2

Project the model at the fixed origin \(T\):

\[X_{T+h|T}=\mathbb E_L[X_{T+h}\mid\Omega_T].\]

Keep known observations and innovations; replace future observations by their forecasts and future innovations by zero:

\[\mathbb E_L[\varepsilon_{T+s}\mid\Omega_T]=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{T+s},&s\le0,\\0,&s>0.\end{cases}\]

Calculate the one-step forecast first, then iterate the same recursion.

Solution

Here \(\phi_1=1/2\) and \(\theta_1=-1/3\). Since \(|\phi_1|<1\) and \(|\theta_1|<1\), the representation is causal and fundamental: past innovations are recoverable, while future innovations have zero linear forecast.

1. One-step forecast

At \(T+1\), \(X_T\) and \(\varepsilon_T\) are known:

\[\begin{aligned}X_{T+1|T}&=\mathbb E_L\!\left[\frac12X_T+\varepsilon_{T+1}-\frac13\varepsilon_T\,\middle|\,\Omega_T\right]\\&=\frac12X_T-\frac13\varepsilon_T.\end{aligned}\]

2. Two- and three-step forecasts

For \(h\ge2\), both innovation dates in the model are after \(T\), so their forecasts are zero:

\[\begin{aligned}X_{T+2|T}&=\frac12X_{T+1|T}=\frac14X_T-\frac16\varepsilon_T,\\X_{T+3|T}&=\frac12X_{T+2|T}=\frac18X_T-\frac1{12}\varepsilon_T.\end{aligned}\]

3. General horizon and limit

\[X_{T+h|T}=\frac12X_{T+h-1|T},\qquad h\ge2.\]

Iterating from the one-step forecast gives

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}X_{T+h|T}&=\left(\frac12\right)^{h-1}X_{T+1|T}\\&=\left(\frac12\right)^{h-1}\left(\frac12X_T-\frac13\varepsilon_T\right),\\&\hspace{5em}h\ge1.\end{aligned}}\]

Since \((1/2)^{h-1}\to0\), the forecast converges to the mean:

\[\boxed{\lim_{h\to\infty}X_{T+h|T}=m_X=0.}\]

Question 3Forecast errors and their variance

Formulas to use — Question 3

Start with the definition: write the observation and its forecast at the same date, then subtract.

\[e_{T+h|T}=X_{T+h}-X_{T+h|T}.\]

Keep information known at \(T\); future innovations have zero linear forecast. Factor any observation-minus-forecast difference and identify the previous error.

Variance: innovations at different dates are uncorrelated, so square their coefficients and add.

\[V(a\varepsilon_s+b\varepsilon_r)=(a^2+b^2)\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\qquad s\ne r.\]

Solution

Start from the original model and keep the forecast origin fixed at \(T\):

\[X_t=\frac12X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t-\frac13\varepsilon_{t-1}.\]

1. One-step error: subtract the forecast

At \(T+1\), \(X_T\) and \(\varepsilon_T\) are known. Only \(\varepsilon_{T+1}\) is a future innovation:

\[\begin{aligned}X_{T+1}&=\frac12X_T+\varepsilon_{T+1}-\frac13\varepsilon_T,\\X_{T+1|T}&=\frac12X_T-\frac13\varepsilon_T.\end{aligned}\]

Subtract the entire forecast, including its negative MA term:

\[\begin{aligned}e_{T+1|T}&=X_{T+1}-X_{T+1|T}\\&=\left(\frac12X_T+\varepsilon_{T+1}-\frac13\varepsilon_T\right)\\&\quad-\left(\frac12X_T-\frac13\varepsilon_T\right)\\&=\left(\frac12X_T-\frac12X_T\right)+\varepsilon_{T+1}\\&\quad+\left(-\frac13\varepsilon_T+\frac13\varepsilon_T\right)\\&=\varepsilon_{T+1}.\end{aligned}\]

The known terms cancel. This gives the starting error \(e_{T+1|T}=\varepsilon_{T+1}\).

2. Derive the recursion before going further

For \(h\ge2\), write the model at \(T+h\):

\[X_{T+h}=\frac12X_{T+h-1}+\varepsilon_{T+h}-\frac13\varepsilon_{T+h-1}.\]

Both innovation dates are now after \(T\), so both have zero forecast. Replace the future observation by its forecast:

\[X_{T+h|T}=\frac12X_{T+h-1|T},\qquad h\ge2.\]

Subtract and factor out \(1/2\) from the observation-minus-forecast difference:

\[\begin{aligned}e_{T+h|T}&=X_{T+h}-X_{T+h|T}\\&=\frac12X_{T+h-1}-\frac12X_{T+h-1|T}\\&\quad+\varepsilon_{T+h}-\frac13\varepsilon_{T+h-1}\\&=\frac12\underbrace{\left(X_{T+h-1}-X_{T+h-1|T}\right)}_{e_{T+h-1|T}}\\&\quad+\varepsilon_{T+h}-\frac13\varepsilon_{T+h-1}.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}e_{T+h|T}&=\frac12e_{T+h-1|T}+\varepsilon_{T+h}\\&\quad-\frac13\varepsilon_{T+h-1},\qquad h\ge2.\end{aligned}}\]

The bracket is exactly the previous forecast error, formed at the same origin \(T\). We can now use it to obtain each next horizon.

3. Two-step error: substitute the one-step error

\[\begin{aligned}e_{T+2|T}&=\frac12e_{T+1|T}+\varepsilon_{T+2}-\frac13\varepsilon_{T+1}\\&=\frac12\varepsilon_{T+1}+\varepsilon_{T+2}-\frac13\varepsilon_{T+1}\\&=\varepsilon_{T+2}+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)\varepsilon_{T+1}\\&=\varepsilon_{T+2}+\frac16\varepsilon_{T+1}.\end{aligned}\]

4. Three-step error: substitute the two-step error

\[\begin{aligned}e_{T+3|T}&=\frac12e_{T+2|T}+\varepsilon_{T+3}-\frac13\varepsilon_{T+2}\\&=\frac12\left(\varepsilon_{T+2}+\frac16\varepsilon_{T+1}\right)\\&\quad+\varepsilon_{T+3}-\frac13\varepsilon_{T+2}\\&=\varepsilon_{T+3}+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)\varepsilon_{T+2}\\&\quad+\frac12\cdot\frac16\varepsilon_{T+1}\\&=\varepsilon_{T+3}+\frac16\varepsilon_{T+2}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{T+1}.\end{aligned}\]

5. General horizon: connect with the MA weights

The coefficients just obtained are \(1\), \(1/6\) and \(1/12\): these are exactly \(\psi_0\), \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) from Question 1. At each horizon, they multiply the newest innovation, the preceding one, and so on.

For any \(h\ge1\), the MA(∞) representation separates future and known innovations:

\[\begin{aligned}X_{T+h}&=\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}\\&\quad+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j},\\X_{T+h|T}&=\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}.\end{aligned}\]

Subtracting cancels the second sum. The remaining dates are \(T+1,\ldots,T+h\), corresponding to \(j=0,\ldots,h-1\):

\[\boxed{e_{T+h|T}=\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}.}\]

Here \(\psi_0=1\) and \(\psi_j=\frac16(\frac12)^{j-1}\) for \(j\ge1\), as established in Question 1. This single sum collects the errors derived above.

6. Forecast-error variance

\[\boxed{V(e_{T+h|T})=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2.}\]
\[\begin{aligned}h=1:\quad V(e_{T+1|T})&=\psi_0^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2=\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\\[6pt]h=2:\quad V(e_{T+2|T})&=(\psi_0^2+\psi_1^2)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\\&=\left(1+\frac1{36}\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\\[6pt]h=3:\quad V(e_{T+3|T})&=(\psi_0^2+\psi_1^2+\psi_2^2)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\\&=\left(1+\frac1{36}+\frac1{144}\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\end{aligned}\]

Each additional horizon adds one squared MA weight:

\[V(e_{T+h|T})=V(e_{T+h-1|T})+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\psi_{h-1}^2,\qquad h\ge2.\]

Using \(\psi_j=\frac16(\frac12)^{j-1}\), the general variance is

\[V(e_{T+h|T})=\left[1+\frac1{36}\sum_{j=1}^{h-1}\left(\frac14\right)^{j-1}\right]\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

For \(h=1\), the sum is empty. As \(h\to\infty\), the geometric sum tends to \(1/(1-1/4)=4/3\):

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\lim_{h\to\infty}V(e_{T+h|T})&=\left(1+\frac1{36}\cdot\frac43\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\\&=\frac{28}{27}\sigma_\varepsilon^2=V(X_t).\end{aligned}}\]

Question 4Prediction interval

Formulas to use — Question 4

Combine the previous two results: forecast ± critical value × forecast-error standard deviation.

\[X_{T+h|T}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{V(e_{T+h|T})}.\]

Here \(P(Z\le z_{1-\alpha/2})=1-\alpha/2\) for \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\). At long horizons, substitute the limits of the forecast and variance.

Solution

Using the forecast from Question 2 and the variance from Question 3, the \((1-\alpha)\)-level prediction interval is

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{V(e_{T+h|T})}.}\]

This normal-quantile interval requires Gaussian innovations or an appropriate normal approximation; weak white noise alone is not sufficient.

Since \(X_{T+h|T}\to0\) and \(V(e_{T+h|T})\to\frac{28}{27}\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), its limiting form is

\[\boxed{0\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{28}{27}\sigma_\varepsilon^2}.}\]

Question 5Impulse responses and the cumulative effect

Formulas to use — Question 5

Unit-shock response: read the coefficient of the innovation at the shock date.

\[\operatorname{IRF}(h)=\frac{\partial X_{t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}=\psi_h.\]

Cumulative response: add the responses, including the impact at \(h=0\).

\[\operatorname{CIRF}(H)=\sum_{h=0}^{H}\psi_h,\qquad\operatorname{CIRF}(\infty)=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\psi_h.\]

Use the MA coefficients from Question 1 and the geometric-series formula.

Solution

From Question 1, \(\psi_0=1\) and \(\psi_h=\frac16(\frac12)^{h-1}\) for \(h\ge1\). In \(X_{t+h}\), the shock \(\varepsilon_t\) has coefficient \(\psi_h\), so

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{IRF}(0)&=\psi_0=1,\\\operatorname{IRF}(1)&=\psi_1=\frac16,\\\operatorname{IRF}(2)&=\psi_2=\frac16\cdot\frac12=\frac1{12},\\\operatorname{IRF}(3)&=\psi_3=\frac16\left(\frac12\right)^2=\frac1{24}.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\operatorname{IRF}(h)&=\frac16\left(\frac12\right)^{h-1},\quad h\ge1,\\\lim_{h\to\infty}\operatorname{IRF}(h)&=0.\end{aligned}}\]

For the long-run cumulative response, include the initial impact \(\psi_0=1\):

\[\begin{aligned}\lim_{H\to\infty}\operatorname{CIRF}(H)&=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{IRF}(h)\\&=1+\frac16\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac12\right)^{h-1}\\&=1+\frac16\frac1{1-1/2}.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\operatorname{CIRF}(\infty)=\frac43.}\]

The response at a distant horizon tends to zero; the total response accumulated across all horizons is \(4/3\).
