LECTURE 6

Structural Vector Autoregressive Models (SVARs)

Theory

Reduced form and identification

\[\begin{aligned}BY_t&=A_0+A_1Y_{t-1}+\epsilon_t,\\Y_t&=B^{-1}A_0+B^{-1}A_1Y_{t-1}+B^{-1}\epsilon_t\\&=\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boldsymbol\mu=B^{-1}A_0,\quad\Phi=B^{-1}A_1,\quad S=B^{-1},\quad\varepsilon_t=S\epsilon_t.\]

\(B\) is the contemporaneous structural matrix; \(S\) is the impact matrix. Structural shocks \(\epsilon_t\) are orthogonal with positive diagonal covariance \(\Omega_\epsilon\).

\[\Sigma_\varepsilon=S\Omega_\epsilon S'.\]

The reduced form does not uniquely identify the structural model. In the bivariate case, normalizing the diagonal of B to 1 leaves 10 structural parameters: 2 contemporaneous coefficients, 2 constants, 4 lag coefficients and 2 shock variances. The reduced form supplies 9: 2 constants, 4 lag coefficients and 3 covariance entries.

With unit-variance shocks, \(S\) has \(n^2\) entries, while the symmetric covariance supplies \(n(n+1)/2\) equations. Equivalently, with unit-diagonal \(S\), its off-diagonal entries plus \(n\) variances also total \(n^2\). Thus the additional restriction count is

\[n^2-\frac{n(n+1)}2=\frac{n(n-1)}2.\]

Short-run zeros restrict contemporaneous effects; long-run zeros restrict cumulative effects. Non-recursive zeros and sign restrictions allow other patterns of exclusions or response directions.

Theory

Recursive short-run identification

Two covariance factorizations

For a positive definite innovation covariance, Cholesky factorization fixes shock variances to 1 and uses a lower-triangular impact matrix with positive diagonal:

\[\Sigma_\varepsilon=SS',\quad\Omega_\epsilon=I_n\]
\[S=\begin{pmatrix}s_{11}&0&\cdots&0\\s_{21}&s_{22}&\cdots&0\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\s_{n1}&s_{n2}&\cdots&s_{nn}\end{pmatrix}.\]

The triangular (LDL) factorization instead fixes the impact diagonal to 1 and leaves shock variances in a diagonal matrix:

\[S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\s_{21}&1&\cdots&0\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\s_{n1}&s_{n2}&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\Omega_\epsilon=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1^2,\ldots,\omega_n^2).\]
\[\Sigma_\varepsilon=S\Omega_\epsilon S'.\]

Each factorization is unique under its stated normalization and fixed variable ordering. Unit-variance shocks measure responses to one standard deviation; unit-diagonal normalization measures an immediate own-variable effect of one unit.

Ordering and contemporaneous effects

\[y_{1t}\longrightarrow y_{2t}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow y_{nt}.\]
\[B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\\star&1&\cdots&0\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\\star&\star&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\]
\[S=B^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\\star&1&\cdots&0\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\\star&\star&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Under this unit-diagonal normalization, row \(i\) of \(B\) describes contemporaneous relations in equation \(i\). Variable \(i\) may respond immediately to variables ordered before it. Column \(j\) of \(S\) gives the impact of shock \(j\): it can affect variables \(j,\ldots,n\), but not earlier variables.

The restrictions concern \(h=0\). Dynamic interactions for \(h\ge1\) remain governed by the unrestricted lag coefficients. Changing the ordering generally changes the shocks and their IRFs.

Recovering the structural shocks

\[\varepsilon_t=S\epsilon_t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\epsilon_t=S^{-1}\varepsilon_t=B\varepsilon_t.\]

\(S\) maps structural shocks into their impact; \(S^{-1}\) recovers shocks from reduced-form innovations by removing the specified contemporaneous effects.

Recursive ordering needs an economic or institutional justification. It can be restrictive when several variables react within the observation period. The reduced form cannot establish which ordering is economically correct.

Theory

Structural impulse responses

Substitute the structural shocks into the reduced-form VMA:

\[\begin{aligned}Y_t&=\boldsymbol m+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Psi_k\varepsilon_{t-k}\\&=\boldsymbol m+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Psi_kS\epsilon_{t-k}\\&=\boldsymbol m+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Theta_k\epsilon_{t-k}.\end{aligned}\]
\[\Theta_k=\Psi_kS\]
\[\Psi_0=I_n\]
\[\Theta_0=S.\]

\(\Psi_k\) is the coefficient of \(L^k\) in \(\Phi(L)^{-1}\). For VAR(1), \(\Psi_h=\Phi^h\), hence \(\Theta_h=\Phi^hS\).

\[\operatorname{IRF}(h)\big|_{\epsilon_j}^{y_i}=\frac{\partial y_{i,t+h}}{\partial\epsilon_{j,t}}=\theta_{ij}^{(h)}.\]

Row \(i\) is the responding variable; column \(j\) is the structural shock. These responses use a unit shock in the chosen normalization.

\[\operatorname{CIRF}(h)=\sum_{k=0}^{h}\Theta_k=\sum_{k=0}^{h}\Phi^kS\quad\text{for VAR(1)}.\]
Theory

Forecast-error variance decomposition

\[e_{T+h|T}=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Theta_k\epsilon_{T+h-k}.\]

Only future shocks remain in the forecast error. Innovations at different dates are uncorrelated, so cross-date terms vanish when taking its covariance:

\[\begin{aligned}\Sigma_e(h)&=\mathbb E(e_{T+h|T}e_{T+h|T}')\\&=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Theta_k\mathbb E(\epsilon_{T+h-k}\epsilon_{T+h-k}')\Theta_k'\\&=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Theta_k\Omega_\epsilon\Theta_k'.\end{aligned}\]
\[\Theta_0\Omega_\epsilon\Theta_0'=S\Omega_\epsilon S'=\Sigma_\varepsilon.\]

For standardized orthogonal shocks, \(\Omega_\epsilon=I_n\):

\[\Sigma_e(h)=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Theta_k\Theta_k'.\]
\[\operatorname{FEVD}(h)\big|_{\epsilon_j}^{y_i}=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}(\theta_{ij}^{(k)})^2}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}(\theta_{i\ell}^{(k)})^2}.\]

The numerator is shock \(j\)’s contribution to variable \(i\)’s forecast-error variance; the denominator adds all shocks. Orthogonal shocks need not have variance 1: with diagonal \(\Omega_\epsilon\ne I_n\), each squared response must retain its shock-variance weight.

Theory

Historical decomposition

Historical decomposition separates the observed path into deterministic terms, initial conditions and contributions of realized structural shocks. For VAR(1), successive substitution gives

\[Y_1=\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi Y_0+\varepsilon_1.\]
\[\begin{aligned}Y_2&=\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi(\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi Y_0+\varepsilon_1)+\varepsilon_2\\&=(I_n+\Phi)\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi^2Y_0+\Phi\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2.\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}Y_3&=\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi Y_2+\varepsilon_3\\&=(I_n+\Phi+\Phi^2)\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi^3Y_0\\&\quad+\Phi^2\varepsilon_1+\Phi\varepsilon_2+\varepsilon_3.\end{aligned}\]
\[Y_t=\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\Phi^k\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi^tY_0+\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\Phi^k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

Using \(\varepsilon_t=S\epsilon_t\) and \(\Theta_k=\Phi^kS\):

\[\begin{aligned}Y_t={}&\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\Phi^k\boldsymbol\mu}_{\text{Constant}}+\underbrace{\Phi^tY_0}_{\text{Initial conditions}}\\&+\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\Theta_k\epsilon_{t-k}}_{\text{Structural shocks}}.\end{aligned}\]

Under stability, the constant component tends to \(\boldsymbol m\) and the initial-condition component tends to zero. Shock \(j\)’s contribution to variable \(i\) is

\[\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\theta_{ij}^{(k)}\epsilon_{j,t-k}.\]

For sufficiently large t, the remaining deterministic difference is negligible, giving the approximation around the unconditional mean:

\[y_{i,t}-m_i\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\theta_{ij}^{(k)}\epsilon_{j,t-k}.\]

This final expression is asymptotic; the finite-sample decomposition above retains the initial conditions.
