LECTURE 4

Forecasting

Definition

Forecasting framework

A forecast combines a model, an information set, an origin \(T\), and a horizon \(h>0\). The target is the future observation \(X_{T+h}\).

Origin and information

\[\Omega_T=\{X_T,X_{T-1},\ldots\}.\]

A one-step forecast targets \(T+1\); an \(h\)-step forecast targets \(T+h\). Multi-step forecasts use the same origin for \(h=1,\ldots,H\).

Causal and fundamental ARMA

\[\Phi(L)X_t=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\]
\[\Phi(L)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jL^j,\]\[\Theta(L)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_kL^k.\]

The innovation process is weak white noise. The causal and fundamental representation lets us use past observations or recoverable past innovations as forecasting information.

\[X_t=m_X+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j},\]
\[m_X=\frac{\mu}{\Phi(1)},\qquad \Psi(L)=\frac{\Theta(L)}{\Phi(L)}.\]\[\psi_0=1.\]

Best linear predictor

The optimal linear forecast is the linear expectation given the information available at \(T\):

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}X_{T+h|T}&=\mathbb E_L[X_{T+h}\mid X_T,X_{T-1},\ldots]\\&=\mathbb E_L[X_{T+h}\mid\varepsilon_T,\varepsilon_{T-1},\ldots].\end{aligned}}\]

It minimizes the mean-squared forecast error among linear predictors:

\[\operatorname{MSE}(h)=\mathbb E[(X_{T+h}-X_{T+h|T})^2].\]
\[e_{T+h|T}=X_{T+h}-X_{T+h|T},\]
\[\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})=\operatorname V(X_{T+h}-X_{T+h|T}).\]
Representation

Forecasts from the MA(∞) representation

Separate innovations by whether they occur before or after the forecast origin:

\[\begin{aligned}X_{T+h}=m_X&+\underbrace{\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}}_{\text{known at }T}\\&+\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}}_{\text{future innovations}}.\end{aligned}\]

For \(j\ge h\), the innovation date \(T+h-j\) is at most \(T\). The future innovations have zero linear forecast.

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}=m_X+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}.}\]

Subtracting this forecast cancels all terms known at \(T\):

\[\boxed{e_{T+h|T}=\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}.}\]

Uncorrelated innovations leave only the squared coefficients in the variance:

\[\boxed{\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2.}\]

These representations apply to the causal and fundamental AR, MA and ARMA models below.

AR models

Autoregressive forecasts

Assume iid Gaussian innovations with mean zero and variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\).

Stationary AR(1)

\[X_t=\mu+\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad |\phi|<1.\]

Iterate the equation forward:

\[X_{T+1}=\mu+\phi X_T+\varepsilon_{T+1},\]
\[\begin{aligned}X_{T+2}&=\mu(1+\phi)+\phi^2X_T\\&\quad+\varepsilon_{T+2}+\phi\varepsilon_{T+1},\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}X_{T+h}&=\mu\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\phi^j+\phi^hX_T\\&\quad+\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\phi^j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(m_X=\mu/(1-\phi)\), the deterministic sum is \(m_X(1-\phi^h)\). Therefore,

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}=m_X+\phi^h(X_T-m_X),\quad h\ge1.}\]

The remaining terms form the error:

\[e_{T+h|T}=\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\phi^j\varepsilon_{T+h-j},\]
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\phi^{2j}\\&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\frac{1-\phi^{2h}}{1-\phi^2}.\end{aligned}}\]

Stationary and causal AR(p)

\[X_t=\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jX_{t-j}+\varepsilon_t.\]

At each horizon, use actual observations for dates already observed and forecasts for later dates:

\[\widetilde X_{T+s|T}=\begin{cases}X_{T+s},&s\le0,\\X_{T+s|T},&s>0.\end{cases}\]

Here the tilde denotes an observed value or a forecast, depending on the date.

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}=\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\widetilde X_{T+h-j|T}.}\]

The MA coefficients used in the error and variance formulas satisfy

\[\psi_0=1,\qquad \psi_j=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(p,j)}\phi_i\psi_{j-i},\quad j\ge1.\]
MA models

Moving-average forecasts

Use the fundamental representation so that the past innovations in the formulas are recoverable from \(\Omega_T\). The MA examples assume iid Gaussian innovations.

MA(1)

\[X_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1},\]
\[X_{T+h}=\mu+\varepsilon_{T+h}+\theta\varepsilon_{T+h-1}.\]

At \(h=1\), the second innovation is \(\varepsilon_T\), which is known. For \(h>1\), both innovations are future shocks.

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}=\begin{cases}\mu+\theta\varepsilon_T,&h=1,\\\mu,&h>1.\end{cases}}\]
\[e_{T+h|T}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{T+1},&h=1,\\\varepsilon_{T+h}+\theta\varepsilon_{T+h-1},&h>1,\end{cases}\]
\[\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})=\begin{cases}\sigma_\varepsilon^2,&h=1,\\\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\theta^2),&h>1.\end{cases}\]

MA(q)

\[X_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

At \(T+h\), only terms with \(k\ge h\) have innovation dates at or before \(T\):

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}=\begin{cases}\mu+\displaystyle\sum_{k=h}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{T+h-k},&h\le q,\\\mu,&h>q.\end{cases}}\]

The forecast returns exactly to \(\mu\) after the MA order. The error and variance are

\[e_{T+h|T}=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\theta_k\varepsilon_{T+h-k},\]
\[\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\theta_k^2.\]

These sums use \(\theta_0=1\) for the current innovation and \(\theta_k=0\) for \(k>q\).

ARMA models

ARMA forecasts

Consider a stationary, causal and fundamental ARMA\((p,q)\), with the iid Gaussian innovations assumed in the forecasting examples:

\[\begin{aligned}X_t&=\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jX_{t-j}\\&\quad+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\end{aligned}\]

Project the equation at \(T+h\). Replace future observations with their forecasts and retain only innovations dated at or before \(T\):

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}X_{T+h|T}&=\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\widetilde X_{T+h-j|T}\\&\quad+\sum_{k=h}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{T+h-k}.\end{aligned}}\]

Use the same observed-or-forecast definition of \(\widetilde X_{T+s|T}\) as for AR\((p)\). The last sum is empty when \(h>q\); the MA component then makes no direct contribution.

Coefficients of the forecast error

In the MA\((\infty)\) representation, the coefficients satisfy

\[\psi_0=1,\]
\[\psi_j=\theta_j+\sum_{i=1}^{\min(p,j)}\phi_i\psi_{j-i},\qquad j\ge1,\]

with \(\theta_j=0\) for \(j>q\). Insert these coefficients into the general forecast-error and variance formulas above.

Uncertainty

Prediction intervals

Under iid Gaussian innovations, the future observation has the conditional distribution:

\[\boxed{X_{T+h}\mid\Omega_T\sim\mathcal N\!\left(X_{T+h|T},\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})\right).}\]

A 95% prediction interval is

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}\ \pm\ 1.96\sqrt{\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})}.}\]

Its centre is the forecast; its width depends on the accumulated squared MA coefficients through the forecast-error variance.

The normal prediction interval requires iid Gaussian innovations; weak white noise alone does not guarantee normality.

Comparison

Random walk and long horizons

Random walk with drift

\[X_t=\mu+X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).\]

The one-step forecast and its continuation are

\[X_{T+1|T}=\mu+X_T,\]
\[X_{T+h|T}=\mu+X_{T+h-1|T},\qquad h>1.\]

Each additional step adds the same drift:

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}=h\mu+X_T.}\]
\[e_{T+1|T}=\varepsilon_{T+1},\]\[e_{T+h|T}=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\varepsilon_{T+j}.\]
\[\boxed{\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})=h\sigma^2.}\]

Here \(\sigma^2\) denotes the innovation variance.

Example

ARMA(1,1) example

\[\begin{aligned}X_t&=\mu+\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1},\\\varepsilon_t&\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).\end{aligned}\]

Assume a stationary, causal and fundamental representation. In this case \(m_X=\mu/(1-\phi)\), \(\psi_0=1\), and

\[\psi_j=(\phi+\theta)\phi^{j-1},\qquad j\ge1.\]

Substituting these coefficients into the MA forecast gives

\[\begin{aligned}X_{T+h|T}&=m_X+\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}\\&=m_X+(\phi+\theta)\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\phi^{j-1}\varepsilon_{T+h-j}.\end{aligned}\]

The error retains the current shock, with coefficient 1:

\[\begin{aligned}e_{T+h|T}&=\varepsilon_{T+h}\\&\quad+(\phi+\theta)\sum_{j=1}^{h-1}\phi^{j-1}\varepsilon_{T+h-j}.\end{aligned}\]
\[\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2.\]

With \(|\phi|<1\), the geometric sum converges:

\[\boxed{\lim_{h\to\infty}\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})=\sigma^2\left[1+\frac{(\phi+\theta)^2}{1-\phi^2}\right].}\]
Definition

Impulse responses and forecasts

An impulse-response function traces the effect of a one-unit innovation at date \(t\) on the subsequent path of \(X\):

\[\boxed{\operatorname{IRF}(h)=\frac{\partial X_{t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_t},\qquad h=0,1,2,\ldots.}\]

The derivative holds the other innovations fixed. A shock can also be measured in standard-deviation units; linearity allows the effects of several shocks to be added.

Wold coefficients

For the causal ARMA representation, or the purely nondeterministic stationary Wold representation,

\[X_{t+h}=m_X+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t+h-j}.\]

Only the term with \(j=h\) contains \(\varepsilon_t\). Therefore,

\[\boxed{\operatorname{IRF}(h)=\psi_h.}\]

The same coefficients determine shock propagation and forecast uncertainty:

\[\boxed{\operatorname V(e_{T+h|T})=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\operatorname{IRF}(j)^2.}\]

Forecast revision

At date \(T\), a newly observed innovation changes the forecast for the same target date \(T+h\):

\[\boxed{X_{T+h|T}-X_{T+h|T-1}=\psi_h\varepsilon_T.}\]

Before \(T\), the term \(\psi_h\varepsilon_T\) was unknown. After observing it, it enters the forecast. For a unit innovation, the revision equals \(\operatorname{IRF}(h)\).

MA models

Moving-average impulse responses

\[X_t=\varepsilon_t+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

A temporary unit shock means \(\varepsilon_t=1\), with all other innovations set to zero in the response calculation.

\[\boxed{\operatorname{IRF}(h)=\psi_h=\begin{cases}\theta_h,&0\le h\le q,\\0,&h>q,\end{cases}}\]

where \(\theta_0=1\). The response sequence is

\[\psi_0=1,\quad\psi_1=\theta_1,\quad\psi_2=\theta_2,\quad\ldots,\quad\psi_q=\theta_q.\]

After horizon \(q\), the shock has no remaining effect. Before the shock date the response is zero.

AR models

Autoregressive impulse responses

AR(1)

\[X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\]\[\Rightarrow\quad X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

For the causal stationary representation,

\[\boxed{\operatorname{IRF}(h)=\psi_h=\phi^h,\qquad h\ge0.}\]

The sequence is \(1,\phi,\phi^2,\phi^3,\ldots\); a temporary innovation can affect many later observations.

AR(2): characteristic-root form

For distinct characteristic roots \(z_1,z_2\) of \(\Phi\), the MA coefficients can be written as:

\[X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\underbrace{\left(\alpha z_1^{-j}+\beta z_2^{-j}\right)}_{\psi_j}\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]
\[\boxed{\operatorname{IRF}(h)=\alpha z_1^{-h}+\beta z_2^{-h},\qquad h\ge1.}\]

The initial response is \(\psi_0=1\). Subsequent responses are obtained by inserting \(h=1,2,\ldots\) into the root expression.

AR(p): dynamic recurrence

At horizon \(h\),

\[X_{t+h}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jX_{t+h-j}+\varepsilon_{t+h}.\]

For \(h\ge1\), differentiate with respect to the initial shock:

\[\frac{\partial X_{t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\frac{\partial X_{t+h-j}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}+\underbrace{\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}}_{0}.\]

With \(\psi_0=1\) and \(\psi_h=0\) for \(h<0\), this gives

\[\boxed{\psi_h=\sum_{j=1}^{\min(p,h)}\phi_j\psi_{h-j},\qquad h\ge1.}\]

For \(h\ge p\), the upper limit is simply \(p\). The IRF follows the same dynamic recurrence as the AR process.

For all three orders, \(\psi_0=1\) and \(\psi_1=\phi_1\).

ARMA models

ARMA impulse responses

At horizon \(h\), the centred dynamics are

\[\begin{aligned}X_{t+h}&=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jX_{t+h-j}\\&\quad+\varepsilon_{t+h}+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{t+h-k}.\end{aligned}\]

Differentiating with respect to \(\varepsilon_t\) gives

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial X_{t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}&=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\frac{\partial X_{t+h-j}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}\\&\quad+\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}\\&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_k\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{t+h-k}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}.\end{aligned}\]

For \(h\ge1\), the current-innovation derivative is zero. In the MA sum, the derivative equals 1 only if \(h=k\), and zero otherwise.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\operatorname{IRF}(h)=\psi_h&=\theta_h+\sum_{j=1}^{\min(p,h)}\phi_j\psi_{h-j},\\&\hspace{3em}h\ge1,\qquad \theta_h=0\text{ for }h>q.\end{aligned}}\]

The initial conditions are \(\psi_0=1\) and \(\psi_h=0\) for \(h<0\). For \(1\le h\le q\), both AR propagation and the direct MA term contribute. When \(h>q\), the direct MA term vanishes:

\[\psi_h=\sum_{j=1}^{\min(p,h)}\phi_j\psi_{h-j}.\]

If also \(h\ge p\), the sum runs from 1 to \(p\). The long-run shape is governed by the AR roots. The MA part mainly determines the initial responses.
