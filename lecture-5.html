LECTURE 5

The Vector Autoregressive Model (VAR)

Definitions

Multivariate time series

A multivariate time series collects \(n\) variables in an \(n\times1\) vector:

\[Y_t=(y_{1t},\ldots,y_{nt})'.\]

Weak white noise

\[\mathbb E(\varepsilon_t)=0\]
\[\mathbb E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-k}')=\begin{cases}\Sigma_\varepsilon,&k=0,\\0,&k\ne0.\end{cases}\]

\(\Sigma_\varepsilon\) is an \(n\times n\) symmetric positive definite matrix. Innovations are uncorrelated across dates; different components may be correlated at the same date.

Weak stationarity and covariance

Weak stationarity requires a constant mean and covariance matrices that depend on the lag, not the date:

\[\mathbb E(Y_t)=\boldsymbol m\]
\[\Gamma_k=\mathbb E[(Y_t-\boldsymbol m)(Y_{t-k}-\boldsymbol m)'].\]
\[\Gamma_0=V(Y_t)\]
\[[\Gamma_k]_{ij}=\gamma_{ij}^{(k)}=\operatorname{Cov}(y_{it},y_{j,t-k}).\]

The diagonal contains autocovariances; off-diagonal entries contain cross-lag covariances. At lag zero, these are variances and contemporaneous covariances.

\[\rho_{ij}^{(k)}=\frac{\gamma_{ij}^{(k)}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{ii}^{(0)}\gamma_{jj}^{(0)}}}.\]
\[\gamma_{ij}^{(k)}=\operatorname{Cov}(y_{i,t+k},y_{jt})=\gamma_{ji}^{(-k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma_{-k}=\Gamma_k'.\]

\(\Gamma_0\) is symmetric. In general, \(\Gamma_k\) is not symmetric when \(k\ne0\).

Representation

The stationary VAR

VAR(p) and lag polynomial

\[Y_t=\boldsymbol\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\Phi_jY_{t-j}+\varepsilon_t.\]

\(Y_t,\boldsymbol\mu,\varepsilon_t\) are \(n\times1\); each \(\Phi_j\) is \(n\times n\). Entry \((i,\ell)\) of \(\Phi_j\) is the coefficient of variable \(\ell\), lagged \(j\) periods, in equation \(i\). All equations use the same lagged variables, with different coefficients.

\[\Phi(L)Y_t=\boldsymbol\mu+\varepsilon_t\]
\[\Phi(L)=I_n-\Phi_1L-\cdots-\Phi_pL^p.\]

Stability and unconditional mean

\[\det(I_n-\Phi_1z-\cdots-\Phi_pz^p)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |z|>1.\]

Equivalently, every eigenvalue of the companion matrix \(F\) lies inside the unit circle:

\[\det(F-\lambda I_{np})=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\lambda|<1.\]

Lag-polynomial roots and companion eigenvalues use reciprocal conventions. Stability gives the causal stationary solution.

\[\boldsymbol m=\boldsymbol\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\Phi_j\boldsymbol m\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boldsymbol m=(I_n-\Phi_1-\cdots-\Phi_p)^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu.\]
\[Y_t-\boldsymbol m=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\Phi_j(Y_{t-j}-\boldsymbol m)+\varepsilon_t.\]

Wold representation

\[\begin{aligned}Y_t&=\Phi(1)^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi(L)^{-1}\varepsilon_t\\&=\boldsymbol m+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Psi_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\end{aligned}\]

\(\Psi_k\) is the \(n\times n\) coefficient of \(L^k\) in \(\Phi(L)^{-1}\), with \(\Psi_0=I_n\). Stability ensures absolute summability of each coefficient sequence.

\[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|\psi_{ij}^{(k)}|<\infty\]
\[i,j=1,\ldots,n.\]

Companion representation

\[X_t=\begin{pmatrix}Y_t-\boldsymbol m\\Y_{t-1}-\boldsymbol m\\\vdots\\Y_{t-p+1}-\boldsymbol m\end{pmatrix}\]
\[\nu_t=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_t\\0\\\vdots\\0\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[F=\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_1&\Phi_2&\cdots&\Phi_p\\I_n&0&\cdots&0\\0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\0&\cdots&I_n&0\end{pmatrix}\]
\[X_t=FX_{t-1}+\nu_t.\]

\(X_t\) and \(\nu_t\) have dimension \(np\times1\); \(F\) has dimension \(np\times np\). The first block row reproduces the VAR; the remaining rows shift the lagged vectors.

\[\Sigma_\nu=\operatorname{diag}(\Sigma_\varepsilon,0,\ldots,0).\]

Yule–Walker equations

Multiply the centered VAR by the lagged centered vector and take expectations. Innovations are orthogonal to past observations:

\[\Gamma_h=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\Phi_j\Gamma_{h-j},\quad h\ge1\]
\[\Gamma_{-h}=\Gamma_h'.\]
\[\Gamma_0=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\Phi_j\Gamma_j'+\Sigma_\varepsilon.\]

Covariance through the companion form

Let \(\Gamma_0^x=\mathbb E(X_tX_t')\). Substitute \(X_t=FX_{t-1}+\nu_t\); the cross terms vanish:

\[\begin{aligned}\mathbb E(X_tX_t')&=\mathbb E[(FX_{t-1}+\nu_t)(FX_{t-1}+\nu_t)']\\&=F\mathbb E(X_{t-1}X_{t-1}')F'+\Sigma_\nu.\end{aligned}\]
\[\Gamma_0^x=F\Gamma_0^xF'+\Sigma_\nu.\]

\(\operatorname{Vec}\) stacks the columns of a matrix into one vector. The Kronecker product \(A\otimes B\) replaces each entry \(a_{ij}\) by the block \(a_{ij}B\). Here, \(F\otimes F\) has dimension \((np)^2\times(np)^2\).

\[\operatorname{Vec}(F\Gamma_0^xF')=(F\otimes F)\operatorname{Vec}(\Gamma_0^x).\]
\[\begin{aligned}{}[I_{(np)^2}-F\otimes F]\operatorname{Vec}(\Gamma_0^x)&=\operatorname{Vec}(\Sigma_\nu),\\\operatorname{Vec}(\Gamma_0^x)&=[I_{(np)^2}-F\otimes F]^{-1}\operatorname{Vec}(\Sigma_\nu).\end{aligned}\]

Reshape the resulting vector column by column. The first block row contains the covariance matrices needed to start the Yule–Walker recursion:

\[\Gamma_0^x=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_0^y&\Gamma_1^y&\cdots&\Gamma_{p-1}^y\\(\Gamma_1^y)'&\Gamma_0^y&\cdots&\Gamma_{p-2}^y\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\(\Gamma_{p-1}^y)'&(\Gamma_{p-2}^y)'&\cdots&\Gamma_0^y\end{pmatrix}.\]

Autocorrelation matrices

\[D_y=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\sqrt{\gamma_{11}^{(0)}},\ldots,\sqrt{\gamma_{nn}^{(0)}}\right)\]
\[R_h^y=D_y^{-1}\Gamma_h^yD_y^{-1}.\]

Dividing each covariance by the two corresponding standard deviations gives the cross-lag correlations.

Dynamics

Main outputs of the VAR

Reduced-form impulse responses

\[Y_t=\boldsymbol m+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Psi_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]
\[\operatorname{IRF}(h)\big|_{\varepsilon_j}^{y_i}=\frac{\partial y_{i,t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_{j,t}}=[\Psi_h]_{ij}.\]

Row \(i\) identifies the responding variable, column \(j\) the shock, and \(h\) the periods since that shock. For a VAR(1), \(\Psi_h=\Phi^h\), a matrix power.

\[\operatorname{CIRF}(h)\big|_{\varepsilon_j}^{y_i}=\sum_{k=0}^{h}[\Psi_k]_{ij}.\]

An IRF measures the response at one horizon; a CIRF adds responses through that horizon. Under stability, \(\Psi_h\to0\), but the accumulated response need not be zero.

Orthogonalized impulse responses

Off-diagonal entries of the innovation covariance describe contemporaneous correlation, not serial correlation. Holding other correlated innovations at zero is mathematically possible, but does not generally isolate a structural shock.

Choose a nonsingular transformation \(Q\) such that \(Q\Sigma_\varepsilon Q'=I_n\). Insert \(Q^{-1}Q\) into the VMA:

\[\begin{aligned}Y_t&=\boldsymbol m+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Psi_k\varepsilon_{t-k}\\&=\boldsymbol m+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Psi_kQ^{-1}Q\varepsilon_{t-k}.\end{aligned}\]
\[w_t=Q\varepsilon_t\]
\[C_k=\Psi_kQ^{-1}\]
\[Y_t=\boldsymbol m+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}C_kw_{t-k}.\]
\[\mathbb E(w_tw_t')=Q\mathbb E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_t')Q'=Q\Sigma_\varepsilon Q'=I_n.\]

The shocks \(w_t\) are uncorrelated and have unit variance. \(Q\) transforms innovations; \(Q^{-1}\) transforms their impact, so \(C_0=Q^{-1}\). The matrix \(Q\) is not unique: orthogonalisation alone does not identify economic shocks.

Forecasting

For a zero-mean or mean-adjusted VAR, future innovations have zero linear forecast. The one-step forecast is

\[Y_{T+1|T}=\Phi_1Y_T+\cdots+\Phi_pY_{T-p+1}.\]

The chain-rule of forecasting substitutes forecasts only for dates beyond the origin:

\[Y_{T+h|T}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\Phi_j\widetilde Y_{T+h-j|T}\]
\[\widetilde Y_{T+s|T}=\begin{cases}Y_{T+s},&s\le0,\\Y_{T+s|T},&s>0.\end{cases}\]
\[\begin{aligned}Y_{T+h}&=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Psi_k\varepsilon_{T+h-k}+\sum_{k=h}^{\infty}\Psi_k\varepsilon_{T+h-k},\\Y_{T+h|T}&=\sum_{k=h}^{\infty}\Psi_k\varepsilon_{T+h-k}.\end{aligned}\]

Known innovations satisfy \(T+h-k\le T\), hence \(k\ge h\). Subtraction leaves only future innovations:

\[e_{T+h|T}=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Psi_k\varepsilon_{T+h-k}\]
\[\Sigma_e(h)=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Psi_k\Sigma_\varepsilon\Psi_k'.\]

Cross-date covariance terms vanish. The diagonal entry \([\Sigma_e(h)]_{ii}\) is variable \(i\)’s forecast-error variance.

Forecast-error variance decomposition

\[\begin{aligned}\Sigma_\varepsilon&=Q^{-1}(Q^{-1})',\\\Sigma_e(h)&=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Psi_kQ^{-1}(Q^{-1})'\Psi_k'\\&=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}C_kC_k'.\end{aligned}\]
\[\operatorname{FEVD}(h)\big|_{\varepsilon_j}^{y_i}=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}(c_{ij}^{(k)})^2}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}(c_{i\ell}^{(k)})^2}.\]

The numerator is shock \(j\)’s contribution; the denominator is total forecast uncertainty for variable \(i\). Here shock labels refer to the orthogonalized, unit-variance shocks. FEVD gives a variance share, unlike IRF and CIRF, which measure responses.

Orthogonality alone does not imply unit variance. For two orthogonal shocks \(u_t,v_t\) with variances \(\sigma_u^2,\sigma_v^2\), the VAR(1) variance retains these weights:

\[V(e_{y,T+h|T})=\sigma_u^2\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}([\Phi^k]_{11})^2+\sigma_v^2\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}([\Phi^k]_{12})^2.\]
\[\operatorname{FEVD}(h)\big|_u^y=\frac{\sigma_u^2\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}([\Phi^k]_{11})^2}{V(e_{y,T+h|T})}\]
\[\operatorname{FEVD}(h)\big|_v^y=\frac{\sigma_v^2\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}([\Phi^k]_{12})^2}{V(e_{y,T+h|T})}.\]
Identification

Structural VAR

The contemporaneous structural matrix \(B\) describes relations among variables at the same date. Structural shocks \(\epsilon_t\) have diagonal covariance \(\Omega_\epsilon\); reduced-form innovations are written \(\varepsilon_t\).

\[\begin{aligned}BY_t&=A_0+A_1Y_{t-1}+\epsilon_t,\\Y_t&=B^{-1}A_0+B^{-1}A_1Y_{t-1}+B^{-1}\epsilon_t\\&=\boldsymbol\mu+\Phi Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boldsymbol\mu=B^{-1}A_0\]
\[\Phi=B^{-1}A_1\]
\[\varepsilon_t=B^{-1}\epsilon_t.\]

This requires \(B\) to be nonsingular. Its inverse transmits contemporaneous feedback. Reduced-form innovations mix structural shocks and are generally correlated:

\[\Sigma_\varepsilon=B^{-1}\mathbb E(\epsilon_t\epsilon_t')(B^{-1})'=B^{-1}\Omega_\epsilon(B^{-1})'.\]

For a bivariate model with unit diagonal in B, the structural model has 2 contemporaneous coefficients, 2 constants, 4 lag coefficients and 2 shock variances: 10 parameters. The reduced form has 2 constants, 4 lag coefficients and 3 distinct covariance entries: 9 parameters. Additional restrictions are needed to recover the structural model.

Data identify the reduced form model. Economic assumptions identify the structural model.
