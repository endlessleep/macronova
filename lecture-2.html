Univariate Time Series: Fundamental Concepts and Tools

Definitions

Linear prediction & innovation

Let \(X_t\) be a zero-mean weakly stationary process. Its linear past is the space generated by past observations:

\[H_{t-1}=\operatorname{span}\{X_{t-1},X_{t-2},\ldots\}.\]

With infinitely many lags, the linear past is understood to include mean-square limits of these linear combinations.

Best linear prediction

\[X_t^\star=E_L(X_t\mid H_{t-1})\]\[=P(X_t\mid H_{t-1}).\]

This is the linear expectation conditional on the past: the linear combination of past observations that minimizes mean squared prediction error.

The course shorthand \(\alpha^{\prime}H_{t-1}\) refers to that weighted linear combination; \(\alpha\) contains the prediction coefficients.

Definition — innovation
\[\varepsilon_t=X_t-X_t^\star\]\[=X_t-P(X_t\mid H_{t-1}).\]

The innovation is the part of the current value that cannot be linearly predicted from its past.

Orthogonality to the past

\[\varepsilon_t\perp H_{t-1},\]\[E(\varepsilon_tX_{t-k})=0\quad(k\ge1).\]

The prediction error is uncorrelated with every past observation. The innovation sequence also has zero mean and is serially uncorrelated:

\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\]\[E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-k})=0\quad(k\ne0).\]
Interpretation
\[X_t=X_t^\star+\varepsilon_t\]

Predictable component + unpredictable innovation. “Unpredictable” here means unpredictable using a linear combination of the past.

Definition & example

Deterministic processes

Definition
\[E_L(X_t\mid H_{t-1})=X_t\]

A covariance-stationary process is linearly deterministic when its current value can be recovered exactly from its linear past.

\[\varepsilon_t=X_t-X_t^\star=0.\]

Random, yet perfectly predictable

\[\kappa_t=A\cos(t)+B\sin(t),\]\[A,B\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,1).\]

The course example satisfies a deterministic recurrence involving its past values. Its current value is therefore perfectly recoverable from the past, even though its coefficients are random.

Common mistake

Linearly deterministic does not mean non-random. It means there is no one-step linear prediction error.

Representation

Wold decomposition

A zero-mean covariance-stationary process has a Wold decomposition:

\[X_t=\kappa_t+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]
\[\psi_0=1,\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j^2<\infty.\]
Correction to the supplied text

Wold’s general theorem guarantees square-summable coefficients. The supplied text says “absolutely summable”, which is a stronger condition and is not guaranteed in general. Reference: Wold decomposition.

Deterministic component

\[\kappa_t\]

The purely deterministic part is recoverable from its past and is uncorrelated with the innovations.

Nondeterministic component

\[\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\]

The remaining component is represented by current and past innovations.

Purely nondeterministic process

If \(\kappa_t=0\), the representation becomes

\[X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

This is an MA\((\infty)\) representation of the process’s linear second-order dynamics.

Interpretation

Wold innovations are prediction errors. They should not automatically be interpreted as structural economic shocks.

Model

ARMA models & lag notation

An ARMA\((p,q)\) model combines \(p\) lagged observations and \(q\) lagged shocks:

\(X_t=c+\phi_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pX_{t-p}\) \({}+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}\) \({}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.\)
\[\Phi(L)X_t=c+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

Autoregressive polynomial

\[\Phi(L)=1-\phi_1L-\cdots-\phi_pL^p.\]

Moving-average polynomial

\[\Theta(L)=1+\theta_1L+\cdots+\theta_qL^q.\]
Common mistake

Preserve the signs: the AR terms move to the left, giving minus signs in the polynomial. The MA polynomial here uses plus signs.

Definitions

Lag, difference & lead operators

Lag operator

The lag operator moves the date backward:

\[LX_t=X_{t-1},\qquad L^2X_t=X_{t-2},\]\[L^kX_t=X_{t-k}.\]

For example,

\[\phi LX_t=\phi X_{t-1}.\]

Difference operator

\[\Delta X_t=X_t-X_{t-1}\]\[=X_t-LX_t=(1-L)X_t.\]
\[\Delta=1-L,\qquad\Delta^k=(1-L)^k.\]

Lead operator

The lead operator moves the date forward:

\[FX_t=X_{t+1},\qquad F^kX_t=X_{t+k}.\]

Lag and lead are inverses:

\[FL=LF=1,\qquad L^{-k}=F^k,\]\[L^{-k}X_t=X_{t+k}.\]
Calculation rules

Lag polynomials & operations

Start with an ordinary polynomial and replace its argument by the lag operator:

\[P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_kz^k,\]\[P(L)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_kL^k.\]

Applied to the process, each power shifts its time index:

\[P(L)X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_kL^kX_t\]\[=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_kX_{t-k}.\]
\[(1-\phi L)X_t=X_t-\phi X_{t-1}.\]

Scalar multiplication and addition

The same coefficient rules apply to lag polynomials and to the lag-series notation used in the course:

\[\alpha A(L)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha a_kL^k,\]\[A(L)+B(L)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(a_k+b_k)L^k.\]

Multiplication

\[A(L)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_kL^k,\]\[B(L)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_kL^k,\]\[A(L)B(L)=B(L)A(L).\]

The coefficient of \(L^k\) in the product collects all pairs of powers adding up to \(k\):

\[c_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_jb_{k-j}.\]
Three cases

Inverting a first-order lag polynomial

\(A(L)=1-\rho L,\)\(A(L)^{-1}=(1-\rho L)^{-1}.\)

The stable inverse depends on the modulus \(|\rho|\).

Causal stable inverse

Condition
\[|\rho|<1\]
\[(1-\rho L)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^kL^k\]\[=1+\rho L+\rho^2L^2+\cdots.\]

The expansion uses current and past values only. This is why it is called causal.

Noncausal stable inverse

Condition
\[|\rho|>1\]
\[(1-\rho L)^{-1}=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{-k}F^k.\]

This stable expansion uses the lead operator and therefore involves future values.

Unit-modulus case

\[|\rho|=1\]

There is no absolutely summable stable inverse in this case.

Worked calculation

AR(1) as an MA(∞)

\[X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad|\phi|<1.\]

To obtain the causal MA\((\infty)\) representation, write the model with \(L\), invert the polynomial, then apply each lag to the innovation.

Write the lag polynomial

\[X_t=\phi LX_t+\varepsilon_t,\]\[X_t-\phi LX_t=\varepsilon_t,\]\[(1-\phi L)X_t=\varepsilon_t.\]

Invert and expand

\[X_t=(1-\phi L)^{-1}\varepsilon_t,\]\[(1-\phi L)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi^kL^k,\]\[X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi^kL^k\varepsilon_t.\]

Apply the lag operator

\[L^k\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-k},\]\[X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi^k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]
\(X_t=\varepsilon_t+\phi\varepsilon_{t-1}\) \({}+\phi^2\varepsilon_{t-2}+\phi^3\varepsilon_{t-3}+\cdots.\)
\[X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi^k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]
Root calculations

Difference equations & quadratic roots

A homogeneous linear difference equation of order \(p\) is

\(X_t=a_1X_{t-1}+a_2X_{t-2}\) \({}+\cdots+a_pX_{t-p}.\)
\[A(L)X_t=0,\]\[A(L)=1-a_1L-a_2L^2-\cdots-a_pL^p.\]

The roots of the associated polynomial determine the dynamic stability of the system.

Quadratic equations

For an order-two polynomial, use

\[ax^2+bx+c=0,\qquad a\ne0,\]\[\Delta=b^2-4ac,\]\[x_{1,2}=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}.\]

If \(\Delta<0\), the roots are complex conjugates and are associated with oscillatory dynamics. Here \(\Delta\) denotes the discriminant, not the difference operator.

2026/27

Characteristic roots & stability

For \(A(L)=1-a_1L-\cdots-a_pL^p\), define the associated polynomial

\[A(z)=1-a_1z-a_2z^2-\cdots-a_pz^p.\]

Characteristic equation

2026/27 convention
\[A(z)=0\]

The characteristic roots are \(z_1,\ldots,z_p\).

Inverse characteristic equation

\[B(r)=r^pA\!\left(\frac1r\right).\]
2026/27 convention
\[B(r)=0\]

The inverse characteristic roots are \(r_1,\ldots,r_p\).

Reciprocal roots

\[r_i=z_i^{-1}=\frac1{z_i},\qquad z_i=\frac1{r_i}.\]

The factorization using the inverse roots is

\[A(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}(1-r_iz).\]

Stability conditions

For stable forward dynamics, every root must satisfy the appropriate condition:

Characteristic roots
\[|z_i|>1\quad\text{for every }i\]

Outside the unit circle.

Inverse roots
\[|r_i|<1\quad\text{for every }i\]

Inside the unit circle.

\[|z_i|>1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|r_i|<1.\]

If at least one \(|z_i|<1\), equivalently \(|r_i|>1\), the forward system is generally unstable.

Method

Solve \(A(z)=0\), take reciprocals \(r_i=1/z_i\), then check the modulus of every root. Use either set of roots, with its matching inequality.

Definitions

Autocovariance & ACF

For a stationary process, define the autocovariance by

\[\gamma_X(h)\]\[=E[(X_t-E(X_t))(X_{t-h}-E(X_{t-h}))].\]
\(\gamma_X(h)=E(X_tX_{t-h})\) \({}-E(X_t)E(X_{t-h}).\)

Stationarity gives \(E(X_t)=E(X_{t-h})=\mu\), so

\[\gamma_X(h)=E(X_tX_{t-h})-\mu^2,\]\[\gamma_X(0)=\operatorname{Var}(X_t).\]
Zero-mean case
\[\gamma_X(h)=E(X_tX_{t-h}).\]

Autocorrelation function

For positive variance \(\gamma_X(0)>0\), normalize the autocovariance:

\[\rho_X(h)=\frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)}\]\[=\operatorname{Corr}(X_t,X_{t-h}).\]
\[-1\le\rho_X(h)\le1,\qquad\rho_X(0)=1,\]\[\rho_X(-h)=\rho_X(h).\]
Application

ACF of an AR(1)

\[X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad|\phi|<1,\]\[\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2).\]

For the stationary causal solution, the variance and autocovariance are

\[\gamma_X(0)=\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2},\]\[\gamma_X(h)=\phi^{|h|}\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2}.\]

Divide the autocovariance by the variance:

\[\rho_X(h)=\frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)}\]\[=\frac{\phi^{|h|}\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)}\]
\[\rho_X(h)=\phi^{|h|}.\]
\[\rho_X(0)=1,\quad\rho_X(1)=\phi,\quad\rho_X(2)=\phi^2.\]

For \(h\ge0\), \(\rho_X(h)=\phi^h\). The ACF follows successive powers of \(\phi\); it does not generally cut off immediately after lag one.

Definitions

PACF & linear projections

The PACF at lag \(h\) measures the linear relationship between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-h}\) after removing the linear effects of the intermediate observations:

\[X_{t-1},X_{t-2},\ldots,X_{t-h+1}.\]

Residualize both observations

\(\widetilde X_t=X_t\) \({}-E_L(X_t\mid X_{t-1},\ldots,X_{t-h+1}),\)
\(\widetilde X_{t-h}=X_{t-h}\) \({}-E_L(X_{t-h}\mid X_{t-1},\ldots,X_{t-h+1}).\)
\[\alpha_X(h)=\operatorname{Corr}(\widetilde X_t,\widetilde X_{t-h}).\]
Interpretation

The ACF measures the total linear relationship at the lag. The PACF measures the remaining relationship after controlling for the observations in between.

PACF as a projection coefficient

For the zero-mean process, project \(X_t\) onto its first \(h\) lags:

\(X_t=a_{h1}X_{t-1}+a_{h2}X_{t-2}\) \({}+\cdots+a_{hh}X_{t-h}+u_t^{(h)}.\)

Here \(u_t^{(h)}\) is the projection residual. The coefficient on the last lag is

\[\alpha_X(h)=a_{hh}.\]

Reading the indices

\[X_t=a_{21}X_{t-1}+a_{22}X_{t-2}+u_t^{(2)}.\]

\(a_{21}\) multiplies \(X_{t-1}\); \(a_{22}\) multiplies \(X_{t-2}\). Therefore \(\alpha_X(2)=a_{22}\).

Common mistake

\(a_{22}\) does not mean “row 2, column 2”. The first index says the projection uses lags up to order 2; the second index identifies the lag whose coefficient is being read.

\[\alpha_X(0)=1\quad\text{by convention},\]\[\alpha_X(1)=a_{11}=\rho_X(1).\]
Method

The autocorrelation matrix & Cramer’s rule

The projection coefficients satisfy the linear system

\[R_ha_h=\rho_h.\]
\[a_h=\begin{pmatrix}a_{h1}\\a_{h2}\\\vdots\\a_{hh}\end{pmatrix}\]
\[\rho_h=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\\\vdots\\\rho_X(h)\end{pmatrix}\]

The autocorrelation matrix is

\[R_h=\]
\[\begin{pmatrix}1\!&\!\rho_X(1)\!&\!\cdots\!&\!\rho_X(h-1)\\\rho_X(1)\!&\!1\!&\!\cdots\!&\!\rho_X(h-2)\\\vdots\!&\!\vdots\!&\!\ddots\!&\!\vdots\\\rho_X(h-1)\!&\!\rho_X(h-2)\!&\!\cdots\!&\!1\end{pmatrix}.\]

We only need its last projection coefficient, \(a_{hh}\), to obtain the PACF.

Cramer’s rule

When \(|R_h|\ne0\), replace the last column of \(R_h\) by \(\rho_h\), giving \(R_h^\star\). Then

\[\alpha_X(h)=a_{hh}=\frac{|R_h^\star|}{|R_h|}.\]

Here \(|R|\) denotes a determinant.

Why the last column?

In \(Ax=b\), Cramer’s rule replaces column \(j\) by \(b\) to find unknown \(x_j\). The PACF is \(a_{hh}\), the \(h\)-th and last unknown, so replace the last column.

Worked calculation

Cramer’s rule at lag two

At lag 2, the unknown vector is \(a_2=(a_{21},a_{22})^{\prime}\). The system is

\[\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{21}\\a_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\end{pmatrix}.\]

We want \(\alpha_X(2)=a_{22}\), so replace column 2:

\[R_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&1\end{pmatrix}\]
\[R_2^\star=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&\rho_X(2)\end{pmatrix}\]
\[a_{22}=\frac{|R_2^\star|}{|R_2|}.\]

For a 2 × 2 matrix, multiply along the diagonals and subtract:

\[\begin{vmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{vmatrix}=ad-bc.\]
\[|R_2^\star|=1\times\rho_X(2)-\rho_X(1)\times\rho_X(1)\]\[=\rho_X(2)-[\rho_X(1)]^2,\]\[|R_2|=1\times1-\rho_X(1)\times\rho_X(1)\]\[=1-[\rho_X(1)]^2.\]
\(\alpha_X(2)=a_{22}\) \(=\dfrac{\rho_X(2)-[\rho_X(1)]^2}{1-[\rho_X(1)]^2}.\)
Application

PACF of an AR(1)

For the stable causal AR(1), \(\rho_X(h)=\phi^h\) for \(h\ge0\). At lag one,

\[\alpha_X(1)=\rho_X(1)=\phi.\]

At lag two, substitute the known ACF values:

\[\alpha_X(2)=\frac{\rho_X(2)-[\rho_X(1)]^2}{1-[\rho_X(1)]^2}\]\[=\frac{\phi^2-\phi^2}{1-\phi^2}=0.\]
\(\alpha_X(1)=\phi,\)\(\alpha_X(h)=0\quad(h\ge2).\)

The PACF cuts off after lag 1.

AR(1) ACF
\[\rho_X(h)=\phi^{|h|}\]

Successive powers: gradual decay in magnitude.

AR(1) PACF

The first lag has coefficient \(\phi\). All later partial correlations are zero.

Comparison

ACF versus PACF

ACF — total linear relationship

\[\rho_X(h)=\operatorname{Corr}(X_t,X_{t-h})\]

The overall linear correlation between observations at a given distance.

PACF — after controlling for intermediate lags

\[\alpha_X(h)=\operatorname{Corr}(\widetilde X_t,\widetilde X_{t-h})\]

The correlation between the residualized observations. Conditioning here means removing linear effects.

Which lags are controlled for?

\(h=1\): none, so \(\alpha_X(1)=\rho_X(1)\).

\(h=2\): control for \(X_{t-1}\).

\(h=3\): control for \(X_{t-1}\) and \(X_{t-2}\).
