PROBLEM SET 1

Exercise 1

An AR(1) process

Consider the process

\[X_t=0.75X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\]

where \(\varepsilon_t\) is a weak white noise.

  1. Write the autoregressive lag polynomial.
  2. Determine the characteristic equation, the inverse characteristic equation, and their roots.
  3. Find the inverse lag polynomial and the MA\((\infty)\) representation.
  4. Compute the ACF and PACF up to \(h=2\).

Given information & notation

For the weak white noise,

\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\] \[\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_{t-h})=0\quad(h\ne0).\]

The lag operator is \(LX_t=X_{t-1}\). For the moment calculations from the MA\((\infty)\) representation, write \(\phi=0.75\).

Question 1 Autoregressive lag polynomial

What to find · Formula

Find the polynomial multiplying \(X_t\).

\[\Phi(L)X_t=\varepsilon_t,\qquad LX_t=X_{t-1}.\]

Use \(L\) to move the lagged term to the left:

\[X_t-0.75LX_t=\varepsilon_t\] \[(1-0.75L)X_t=\varepsilon_t.\]
\[\boxed{\Phi(L)=1-0.75L}\]

Question 2 Characteristic equations & roots

Characteristic equation

What to find · Formula
\[\Phi(z)=0\]

Replace the lag operator by \(z\), then solve for the characteristic root \(z_1\).

Set the lag polynomial equal to zero:

\[\Phi(z)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 1-0.75z=0\] \[0.75z=1,\] \[z_1=\frac{1}{0.75}=\frac43\simeq1.33.\]

Inverse characteristic equation

What to find · Formula
\[r\Phi(r^{-1})=0\]

Find the inverse characteristic root \(r_1\). Multiplying by \(r\) removes the negative power and gives a root equal to \(1/z_1\).

For an AR(1), multiply \(\Phi(r^{-1})\) by \(r\) to remove \(r^{-1}\):

\[r\Phi(r^{-1})=0\] \[r(1-0.75r^{-1})=0\] \[r-0.75=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_1=0.75.\]
\[|z_1|>1,\qquad r_1=\frac1{z_1},\qquad |r_1|<1.\]

The characteristic root lies outside the unit circle; the inverse root lies inside. The characteristic-root condition \(|z_1|>1\), or equivalently the inverse-root condition \(|r_1|<1\), shows that \(\Phi(L)\) is invertible.

Question 3 Inverse polynomial & MA\((\infty)\)

What to find · Formula
\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\Phi(L)}\]\[X_t=\Phi(L)^{-1}\varepsilon_t\]

Expand the inverse in powers of \(L\), then express \(X_t\) using current and past innovations.

Since \(|0.75|<1\), expand the inverse using the geometric series:

\[\frac1{1-x}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x^j\quad(|x|<1).\] \[\Phi(L)^{-1}=(1-0.75L)^{-1}\] \[=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(0.75L)^j\] \[\boxed{\Phi(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}0.75^jL^j}.\]

The root calculation explains why this expansion works: the inverse root has modulus below one, so the geometric series converges. Applying the inverse polynomial to the white noise expresses \(X_t\) as a convergent weighted sum of current and past innovations.

For this AR\((1)\), the characteristic-root condition gives an MA\((\infty)\) representation:

\[\boxed{|z_1|>1\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{convergent MA}(\infty)\text{ representation}}\]

Apply this inverse to \(\varepsilon_t\). Each power of the lag operator moves the innovation back by that many periods, so \(L^j\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-j}\):

\[X_t=\Phi(L)^{-1}\varepsilon_t\] \[X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}0.75^jL^j\varepsilon_t.\]
\[\boxed{X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}0.75^j\varepsilon_{t-j}}\]
\(X_t=\varepsilon_t+0.75\varepsilon_{t-1}\)\({}+0.75^2\varepsilon_{t-2}+\cdots.\)

Question 4 ACF & PACF up to \(h=2\)

What to find · Formula

Find the ACF at lags \(0,1,2\): compute the variance and autocovariances, then divide.

\[\rho_X(h)=\frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)}.\]

Here \(\phi=0.75\). The MA representation has zero mean because the innovations have zero mean. Therefore the mean product in the autocovariance vanishes:

\[\gamma_X(h)=E(X_tX_{t-h})\]\[{}-E(X_t)E(X_{t-h})\]\[=E(X_tX_{t-h}).\]

ACF — Method 1: MA\((\infty)\)

Variance

First compute \(\gamma_X(0)\), the denominator of the ACF. Use different indices for the two copies of the MA sum:

\[X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\] \[\gamma_X(0)=E(X_t^2)\] \[=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi^{j+k}E(\varepsilon_{t-j}\varepsilon_{t-k}).\]

For weak white noise, different dates are uncorrelated. Only \(j=k\) remains, and each matching expectation equals \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\):

\[\gamma_X(0)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^{2j}\sigma_\varepsilon^2\] \[=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\phi^2+\phi^4+\cdots)\] \[=\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2}.\]

The last equality is the geometric-series formula, with ratio \(\phi^2<1\).

Autocovariance at lag \(h\ge0\)

Multiply the two MA representations:

\[X_{t-h}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi^k\varepsilon_{t-h-k},\] \[\gamma_X(h)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi^{j+k}\] \[\hspace{1em}{}\times E(\varepsilon_{t-j}\varepsilon_{t-h-k}).\]

Because innovations at different dates are uncorrelated, only terms with the same date contribute:

\[t-j=t-h-k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=j-h.\] \[k\ge0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad j\ge h.\]

Substitute \(k=j-h\) into the power \(j+k\), and keep only matching dates:

\[E(X_tX_{t-h})=\sum_{j=\color{red}{h}}^{\infty}\phi^{2j-h}\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

Lower bound. The sum starts at \(j=h\), not \(j=0\), because \(k=j-h\ge0\).

Writing the first terms makes the pattern of the sum explicit:

\[\gamma_X(h)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(\phi^h+\phi^{h+2}+\phi^{h+4}+\cdots)\] \[=\phi^h\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\phi^2+\phi^4+\cdots).\]

Equivalently, in the indexed sum, factor out \(\phi^{-h}\), then its first remaining term \(\phi^{2h}\). With \(\ell=j-h\), the new index starts at zero:

\[\gamma_X(h)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\phi^{-h}\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\phi^{2j},\] \[\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}\phi^{2j}=\phi^{2h}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\phi^{2\ell}\] \[=\frac{\phi^{2h}}{1-\phi^2}.\] \[\gamma_X(h)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\phi^{-h}\frac{\phi^{2h}}{1-\phi^2}\] \[=\phi^h\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2}.\]

Divide by the variance to obtain the ACF:

\[\rho_X(h)=\frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)}=\phi^h\quad(h\ge0).\]

ACF — Method 2: Yule–Walker / AR recursion

This second calculation obtains the autocovariance recursively from the AR equation. Multiply both sides by \(X_{t-h}\) and take expectations:

\(E(X_tX_{t-h})=0.75E(X_{t-1}X_{t-h})\)\({}+E(\varepsilon_tX_{t-h}).\)

For \(h>0\), \(X_{t-h}\) contains only past innovations, which are uncorrelated with \(\varepsilon_t\). Hence the last term is zero:

\[\gamma_X(h)=0.75\gamma_X(h-1)\] \[\rho_X(h)=0.75\rho_X(h-1).\]

Starting from \(\rho_X(0)=1\),

\[\rho_X(1)=0.75,\] \[\rho_X(2)=0.75\times0.75=0.5625.\]

PACF — Cramer’s rule

What to find · Formula

Find \(\alpha_X(1)\) and \(\alpha_X(2)\) using the ACF values already calculated.

\[\alpha_X(h)=\frac{|\mathcal R_h^\star|}{|\mathcal R_h|}.\]

Cramer’s rule gives the last projection coefficient as the ratio of these two determinants.

The vertical bars denote determinants. To calculate the PACF at lag \(h\), we need the coefficient \(a_{hh}\), the last coefficient in the projection of \(X_t\) on its first \(h\) lags. The entries of \(\mathcal R_h\) are the autocorrelations among the first \(h\) lagged values. The Yule–Walker system for their projection coefficients is \(\mathcal R_h a_h=\rho_h\), where \(a_h=(a_{h1},\ldots,a_{hh})^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\rho_h=(\rho_X(1),\ldots,\rho_X(h))^{\mathsf T}\). By Cramer’s rule, \(a_{hh}\) is obtained by replacing the last column of \(\mathcal R_h\) with \(\rho_h\), because \(a_{hh}\) is the last unknown in the system:

\[\mathcal R_h=\begin{bmatrix} \rho_X(0)&\rho_X(1)&\cdots&\rho_X(h-1)\\ \rho_X(1)&\rho_X(0)&\cdots&\rho_X(h-2)\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ \rho_X(h-1)&\rho_X(h-2)&\cdots&\rho_X(0) \end{bmatrix}\]
\[\mathcal R_h^\star=\begin{bmatrix} \rho_X(0)&\rho_X(1)&\cdots&\rho_X(1)\\ \rho_X(1)&\rho_X(0)&\cdots&\rho_X(2)\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ \rho_X(h-1)&\rho_X(h-2)&\cdots&\rho_X(h) \end{bmatrix}.\]
Lag 1

At lag 1, the PACF equals the ACF:

\[\alpha_X(1)=\rho_X(1)=\phi=0.75.\]
Lag 2

Since \(\rho_X(0)=1\), the two matrices are:

\[\mathcal R_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&1\end{pmatrix}\]
\[\mathcal R_2^\star=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&\rho_X(2)\end{pmatrix}.\]

For each \(2\times2\) determinant, multiply along the main diagonal and subtract the product along the other diagonal:

\[|\mathcal R_2^\star|=1\cdot\rho_X(2)-\rho_X(1)\rho_X(1),\] \[|\mathcal R_2|=1\cdot1-\rho_X(1)\rho_X(1).\]

Substitute \(\rho_X(1)=\phi\) and \(\rho_X(2)=\phi^2\):

\[\alpha_X(2)=\frac{\rho_X(2)-\rho_X(1)^2}{1-\rho_X(1)^2}\] \[=\frac{\phi^2-\phi\phi}{1-\phi\phi}\] \[=\frac{0.5625-0.75^2}{1-0.75^2}=\frac0{0.4375}=0.\]
ACF and PACF up to lag 2
Lag \(h\)012
\(\rho_X(h)\)10.750.5625
\(\alpha_X(h)\)—0.750
Exercise 2

An MA(1) process

Let \(U_t\) be the moving average process of order 1

\[U_t=\varepsilon_t-0.5\varepsilon_{t-1},\]

where \(\varepsilon_t\) is a weak white noise.

  1. Write this model using the lag operator.
  2. Find the inverse characteristic equation associated with the moving average lag polynomial.
  3. Show that
    \[U_t=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}0.5^jU_{t-j}+\varepsilon_t.\]
  4. Compute the ACF and the PACF up to \(h=2\).

Question 1 Moving average lag polynomial

What to find · Formula

Find the polynomial acting on the innovations, keeping the minus sign in the given model.

\[U_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\qquad L\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-1}.\]

Replace the lagged innovation by \(L\varepsilon_t\), then factor out \(\varepsilon_t\):

\[U_t=\varepsilon_t-0.5L\varepsilon_t\] \[=(1-0.5L)\varepsilon_t.\]
\[\Theta(L)=1-0.5L.\]

Question 2 Inverse characteristic equation

What to find · Formula

Find the inverse characteristic equation and its root \(r_1\).

\[r\Theta(r^{-1})=0.\]

Check whether this inverse root lies inside the unit circle: \(|r_1|<1\) is the invertibility condition here.

Substitute \(r^{-1}\) for \(L\), then multiply by \(r\):

\[r\Theta(r^{-1})=0\] \[r(1-0.5r^{-1})=0\] \[r-0.5=0.\]
\[r_1=0.5,\qquad |r_1|<1.\]

The inverse root lies inside the unit circle, so this MA lag polynomial is invertible.

Question 3 AR\((\infty)\) representation

What to find · Formula

Express \(U_t\) using its own past values and the current innovation.

\[\varepsilon_t=\Theta(L)^{-1}U_t,\] \[\frac1{1-x}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x^j\quad(|x|<1).\]

Since \(0.5<1\), the geometric expansion gives:

\[(1-0.5L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}0.5^jL^j.\]

This condition links the previous question to this representation: because the MA polynomial is invertible, its inverse can be expanded as a convergent series in powers of \(L\). Applying that series to \(U_t\) expresses the innovations in terms of current and past values of \(U\); rearranging then gives an AR\((\infty)\) representation.

For this MA\((1)\), invertibility is equivalent to having the convergent AR\((\infty)\) representation obtained from the geometric expansion:

\[\boxed{\text{invertible MA}(1)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\text{convergent AR}(\infty)\text{ representation}}\]

Apply this inverse to \(U_t\):

\[\varepsilon_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}0.5^jL^jU_t\] \[=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}0.5^jU_{t-j}.\]

The \(j=0\) term is \(U_t\). Separate it and move all terms with \(j\ge1\) to the other side:

\[\varepsilon_t=U_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}0.5^jU_{t-j}.\]
\[U_t=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}0.5^jU_{t-j}+\varepsilon_t.\]

The minus sign in this AR representation comes from moving the positive sum to the other side of the equation.

Question 4 Autocovariances, ACF & PACF

What to find · Formula

Find the ACF at lags \(0,1,2\) by computing variance and autocovariances.

\[\gamma_U(h)=\operatorname{Cov}(U_t,U_{t-h}),\] \[\rho_U(h)=\frac{\gamma_U(h)}{\gamma_U(0)}.\]

Rewrite the given model as \(U_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}\), where \(\theta_1=-0.5\). Its mean is zero, so \(\gamma_U(h)=E(U_tU_{t-h})\). At different dates, the white-noise products have zero expectation.

Autocovariances

At \(h=0\), expand the square. The cross term has zero expectation:

\[\gamma_U(0)=E[(\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1})^2]\] \[=E(\varepsilon_t^2)+2\theta_1E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1})\] \[\hspace{1em}{}+\theta_1^2E(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2)\] \[=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\theta_1^2).\]

At \(h=1\), the only date shared by \(U_t\) and \(U_{t-1}\) is \(t-1\). Expand the product:

\[\gamma_U(1)=E(U_tU_{t-1})\] \[=E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1})+\theta_1E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-2})\] \[\hspace{1em}{}+\theta_1E(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2)\] \[\hspace{1em}{}+\theta_1^2E(\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-2})\] \[=\theta_1\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

For \(h>1\), the two MA(1) expressions have no innovation date in common. Hence:

\[\gamma_U(h)=0\quad(h>1).\]

ACF

Divide each autocovariance by \(\gamma_U(0)\):

\[\rho_U(0)=1,\] \[\rho_U(1)=\frac{\theta_1}{1+\theta_1^2},\] \[\rho_U(h)=0\quad(h>1).\]

For the given model, \(\theta_1=-0.5\). Substitution gives:

\[\gamma_U(0)=(1+(-0.5)^2)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\] \[=1.25\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\qquad \gamma_U(1)=-0.5\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\] \[\rho_U(1)=\frac{-0.5}{1+(-0.5)^2}\] \[=\frac{-0.5}{1.25}=-0.4.\]

PACF — Cramer’s rule

What to find · Formula

Find the PACF at lags 1 and 2 from the ACF.

\[\alpha_U(1)=\rho_U(1),\] \[\alpha_U(2)=\frac{\rho_U(2)-\rho_U(1)^2}{1-\rho_U(1)^2}.\]

At lag 1, \(\alpha_U(1)=\rho_U(1)=-0.4\). At lag 2, the coefficient sought is the second and last unknown, so Cramer’s rule replaces the second column of the autocorrelation matrix:

\[\alpha_U(2)=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}1&\rho_U(1)\\\rho_U(1)&\rho_U(2)\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}1&\rho_U(1)\\\rho_U(1)&1\end{vmatrix}}\] \[=\frac{\rho_U(2)-\rho_U(1)^2}{1-\rho_U(1)^2}.\]

Substitute \(\rho_U(2)=0\) and \(\rho_U(1)=-0.4\). Squaring the lag-1 correlation gives \((-0.4)^2=0.16\):

\[\alpha_U(2)=\frac{0-(-0.4)^2}{1-(-0.4)^2}\] \[=-\frac{0.16}{0.84}\simeq-0.19.\]
MA(1) ACF and PACF up to lag 2
Lag \(h\)012
\(\rho_U(h)\)1−0.40
\(\alpha_U(h)\)—−0.4\(-0.19\)

The lag-2 PACF is rounded to two decimal places.
