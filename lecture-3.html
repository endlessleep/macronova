LECTURE 3

ARMA Processes and Their Properties

Part 1

Class of ARMA Processes

Definition of an ARMA(p,q) process

An autoregressive moving-average process combines two sources of dependence: past observations and current or past shocks. At every date \(t\), an ARMA\((p,q)\) satisfies

\[\begin{aligned}X_t={}&\mu+\phi_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pX_{t-p}\\&+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.\end{aligned}\]

The integer \(p\) is the number of autoregressive lags; \(\phi_i\) is the coefficient on the observation \(i\) periods ago. The integer \(q\) is the number of lagged shocks; \(\theta_j\) is the coefficient on the shock \(j\) periods ago. For positive orders, \(\phi_p\ne0\) and \(\theta_q\ne0\), so the stated orders are the actual degrees.

The shock \(\varepsilon_t\) is a weak white noise: it has mean zero, constant finite variance, and zero covariance with shocks at other dates. Throughout the correlation calculations, assume the noise variance is positive.

\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\qquad 0<\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2<\infty,\]\[E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_s)=0\quad(t\ne s).\]

The constant \(\mu\) is the intercept: it adds the same amount to the equation at every date. A model with no constant term uses \(\mu=0\), as in the earlier zero-mean examples. With autoregressive feedback, the long-run mean also reflects the contribution of past observations, so it generally differs from \(\mu\).

Lag-polynomial representation

The lag operator shifts a series back one date: \(LX_t=X_{t-1}\), and \(L^iX_t=X_{t-i}\). Collect the AR terms on the left and the shock terms on the right. This defines two polynomials in \(L\):

\[\Phi(L)=1-\phi_1L-\cdots-\phi_pL^p,\]\[\Theta(L)=1+\theta_1L+\cdots+\theta_qL^q.\]

The minus signs in the AR polynomial come from moving the lagged observations to the left. The MA coefficients keep their signs because the shocks stay on the right. A coefficient itself may be positive or negative.

\[\boxed{\Phi(L)X_t=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.}\]

Subtract the lagged observations from both sides of the expanded equation:

\[\begin{aligned}X_t-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_iX_{t-i}&=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\\\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_iL^i\right)X_t&=\mu+\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^q\theta_jL^j\right)\varepsilon_t.\end{aligned}\]

The two brackets are precisely \(\Phi(L)\) and \(\Theta(L)\). This changes the notation, not the underlying equation.

AR(p) and MA(q) as special cases

Autoregressive process: AR(p)

With no lagged shocks, \(q=0\) and \(\Theta(L)=1\). An AR\((p)\) has a degree-\(p\) AR polynomial, with \(\phi_p\ne0\):

\[X_t=\mu+\phi_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pX_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t.\]
\[\boxed{\Phi(L)X_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t.}\]

Moving-average process: MA(q)

With no lagged observations, \(p=0\) and \(\Phi(L)=1\). An MA\((q)\) is a finite weighted sum of shocks, plus an intercept, with \(\theta_q\ne0\):

\[X_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.\]
\[\boxed{X_t=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.}\]

In both cases the coefficient on the current shock is 1. The words “moving average” refer to a linear combination of shocks; the weights do not have to sum to 1.

The intercept and the mean

Assume a stationary solution exists and \(\Phi(1)\ne0\). Its mean \(m_X=E(X_t)\) is the same at every date. Taking expectations removes every shock term because white noise has mean zero. The AR terms contribute their coefficients multiplied by the same mean.

\[\boxed{m_X=\frac{\mu}{1-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i}=\frac{\mu}{\Phi(1)}.}\]

Here \(\Phi(1)\) means evaluate the polynomial at 1: \(\Phi(1)=1-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\). For an MA\((q)\), \(\Phi(1)=1\), so the mean equals the intercept. For an AR or ARMA process, autoregressive feedback changes the mean.

1. Take expectations term by term

\[E(X_t)=\mu+\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_iE(X_{t-i})+E(\varepsilon_t)+\sum_{j=1}^q\theta_jE(\varepsilon_{t-j}).\]

Stationarity gives \(E(X_{t-i})=m_X\). White noise gives \(E(\varepsilon_{t-j})=0\) for every \(j\), including \(j=0\).

\[m_X=\mu+\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i m_X.\]

2. Collect the terms in the mean

\[m_X-\left(\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\right)m_X=\mu,\]\[\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\right)m_X=\mu,\]\[\Phi(1)m_X=\mu.\]

3. Divide by the nonzero coefficient

The stationarity root condition in Part 2 excludes a root at \(z=1\), so \(\Phi(1)\ne0\). Division is therefore valid:

\[m_X=\frac{\mu}{\Phi(1)}.\]

In particular, \(\mu=0\) gives \(m_X=0\) under these assumptions.

Mean-adjusted ARMA

To study dependence around the mean, remove the constant level from each observation. Define \(\widetilde X_t=X_t-m_X\). This process has mean zero, while its covariance and correlation structure are unchanged.

\[\boxed{\widetilde X_t=X_t-\frac{\mu}{1-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i}=X_t-\frac{\mu}{\Phi(1)}.}\]\[\boxed{\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.}\]

The intercept disappears because it is exactly balanced by the mean contribution of the AR terms. The AR and MA coefficients themselves do not change.

Substitute \(X_t=\widetilde X_t+m_X\) and \(X_{t-i}=\widetilde X_{t-i}+m_X\) into the original model:

\[\widetilde X_t+m_X=\mu+\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i(\widetilde X_{t-i}+m_X)+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]\[\widetilde X_t=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\widetilde X_{t-i}+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t+\left[\mu+\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i m_X-m_X\right].\]

The bracket is zero by the mean equation:

\[\mu+\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i m_X-m_X=\mu-\Phi(1)m_X=0.\]\[\widetilde X_t-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\widetilde X_{t-i}=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]\[\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

Subtracting fixed constants does not affect covariance:

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X_t-m_X,X_s-m_X)=\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_s).\]
Part 2

Stationarity, Stability and Invertibility Conditions

Three questions must be kept separate. Does a stationary solution exist? Can it be constructed from current and past shocks? Can the shocks be recovered from current and past observations? These are the questions of stationarity, stability, and causal invertibility.

Unit circle and root conventions

For a complex number \(z=a+ib\), its modulus \(|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) is its distance from zero. The complex unit circle is the set of points at distance 1:

\[\mathcal C=\{z\in\mathbb C:\ |z|=1\}.\]

A root is a value that makes a polynomial zero. Throughout the root conditions, assume \(\Phi\) and \(\Theta\) have no common roots. A common factor could otherwise cancel in their ratio, so the apparent AR and MA orders would not describe the reduced model.

The AR characteristic equation is \(\Phi(z)=0\). The inverse characteristic equation replaces \(z\) with \(1/r\), then multiplies by \(r^p\) to remove negative powers:

\[\Phi(z)=1-\phi_1z-\cdots-\phi_pz^p=0,\]\[r^p\Phi(1/r)=r^p-\phi_1r^{p-1}-\cdots-\phi_p=0.\]

Their roots are reciprocals: \(r_i=1/z_i\). Thus a root outside the circle corresponds to an inverse root inside it. A root on the circle remains on the circle after inversion.

Because \(\Phi(0)=1\), a characteristic root \(z_i\) is nonzero. Put \(r_i=1/z_i\). Then

\[r_i^p\Phi(1/r_i)=r_i^p\Phi(z_i)=0.\]

Conversely, since \(\phi_p\ne0\), zero is not a root of the inverse polynomial. If \(r_i^p\Phi(1/r_i)=0\), divide by \(r_i^p\) to get \(\Phi(1/r_i)=0\).

\[|r_i|=\frac1{|z_i|},\]\[|z_i|>1\iff|r_i|<1,\qquad |z_i|\ne1\iff|r_i|\ne1.\]

The same construction applies to the degree-\(q\) MA polynomial: solve \(\Theta(w)=0\) or \(s^q\Theta(1/s)=0\), with \(s=1/w\). We use \(w\) for MA roots when both polynomials appear together.

Stationarity: existence and uniqueness

Stationarity condition. Under the no-common-root assumption, a stationary ARMA solution exists exactly when no AR root lies on \(\mathcal C\). The equivalent inverse-root condition is:

\[\boxed{\Phi(z_i)=0\implies |z_i|\ne1\quad\text{for every }i.}\]\[\boxed{r_i^p\Phi(1/r_i)=0\implies |r_i|\ne1\quad\text{for every }i.}\]

Roots may be inside or outside the circle. Excluding the circle guarantees a convergent two-sided filter; restricting all roots to the outside will give the stronger causal solution below.

Theorem — unique stationary solution

Let \(\varepsilon_t\) be weak white noise. Suppose \(\Phi\) and \(\Theta\) have no common roots, and \(\Phi\) has no root on the unit circle. Define the filter \(\Psi(L)=\Theta(L)/\Phi(L)\). The ARMA equation then has the unique stationary solution

\[\boxed{X_t=m_X+\Psi(L)\varepsilon_t,}\]\[\Psi(L)=\frac{\Theta(L)}{\Phi(L)},\qquad m_X=\frac{\mu}{\Phi(1)}.\]

The fraction denotes applying an inverse linear filter, rather than ordinary division of observations. Depending on the AR roots, its expansion may use both lags and leads. A series with positive and negative powers is called a bilateral Laurent series:

\[\Psi(L)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_jL^j,\]\[X_t=m_X+\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Here \(\psi_j\) is the weight on a shock \(j\) periods away. Positive \(j\) uses past shocks, \(j=0\) uses the current shock, and negative \(j\) uses future shocks. With no unit-circle roots, the rational filter has an absolutely summable expansion: the absolute weights have a finite total. The series converges in mean square, meaning that the expected squared error from truncating it tends to zero.

Boundary in the source statement

The supplied stationary-solution remark is written as

\[|z_i|\le1\ \Longrightarrow\ \Psi(L)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_jL^j.\]

The equality case conflicts with the theorem’s assumption: \(|z_i|=1\) is excluded. The relevant situation is an AR root strictly inside the circle, with no AR roots on it. Such roots require future-shock terms; a mixture of inside and outside roots generally requires both directions.

1. Remove the mean and invert the AR polynomial

\[\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\]\[\widetilde X_t=\Phi(L)^{-1}\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t=\Psi(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

To see why the inverse exists, factor \(\Phi(L)\) into first-order factors \((1-r_iL)\), where \(r_i=1/z_i\). For \(|r_i|<1\), a geometric series in lags converges. For \(|r_i|>1\), factor out \(-r_iL\) and use a geometric series in leads:

\[(1-r_iL)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r_i^kL^k\quad(|r_i|<1),\]\[(1-r_iL)^{-1}=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}r_i^{-k}L^{-k}\quad(|r_i|>1).\]

Both coefficient sequences have finite sums of absolute values. Finite products of these filters, followed by the finite polynomial \(\Theta\), remain absolutely summable. No factor has \(|r_i|=1\), which is exactly the excluded boundary. The AR(1) example in Part 3 shows both geometric expansions step by step.

2. Check the moments

Absolute summability implies square summability. White-noise orthogonality then makes the mean-square error of omitted terms equal to their squared weights times \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\); this tends to zero. For the limiting process,

\[E(X_t)=m_X,\]\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}\psi_j^2<\infty.\]\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})&=\sum_{j,k\in\mathbb Z}\psi_j\psi_k E(\varepsilon_{t-j}\varepsilon_{t-h-k})\\&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}\psi_j\psi_{j-h}.\end{aligned}\]

The product survives only when \(t-j=t-h-k\), that is, \(k=j-h\). The covariance depends on \(h\), not on \(t\), so the solution is weakly stationary.

3. Check the equation and uniqueness

\[\begin{aligned}\Phi(L)X_t&=\Phi(1)m_X+\Phi(L)\Psi(L)\varepsilon_t\\&=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\end{aligned}\]

The absolutely summable inverse can be applied in mean square to any stationary finite-variance solution. Doing so fixes its centred part as \(\Psi(L)\varepsilon_t\), while the expectation equation fixes its mean. Hence there is no second stationary solution to the same equation with the same driving noise.

Why every finite MA(q) is stationary

For an MA\((q)\), \(\Phi(L)=1\), so there are no AR roots to restrict. Each observation is the same finite combination of white-noise shocks, shifted in time. Its mean is \(\mu\); its variance is finite, and the overlap of shock dates depends only on the lag.

\[E(X_t)=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j^2,\qquad\theta_0=1.\]

Thus a stationary solution exists for every set of finite MA coefficients, whether or not the MA polynomial is causally invertible. Part 4 derives these moments by matching shock dates.

Stability: a causal representation

An ARMA process is stable when every AR characteristic root lies strictly outside the unit circle. Equivalently, every inverse AR root lies strictly inside it:

\[\boxed{|z_i|>1\iff |r_i|<1\quad\text{for every AR root}.}\]

Each inverse factor now has a convergent expansion in non-negative powers of \(L\). Their product gives a unilateral MA(∞):

\[\Psi(L)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_jL^j.\]
\[\boxed{X_t=m_X+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.}\]

“Causal” means the current observation is constructed using current and past shocks only. Stability therefore gives a stationary causal solution. Stationarity alone also permits solutions that depend on future shocks.

For one factor, multiply a finite geometric sum by \(1-rL\):

\[(1-rL)\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^kL^k=1-r^{n+1}L^{n+1}.\]

When \(|r|<1\), the remainder applied to a stationary finite-variance sequence tends to zero in mean square. For white noise the expected squared magnitude is \(|r|^{2n+2}\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), which tends to zero. Hence the sum acts as the inverse filter.

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\prod_{i=1}^p\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r_i^kL^k\right),\]\[\Psi(L)=\Theta(L)\Phi(L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_jL^j.\]

Only non-negative powers occur when these series are multiplied. Applying \(L^j\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-j}\) gives the causal MA(∞) form. The coefficients can also be found by multiplying \(\Phi(L)\Psi(L)=\Theta(L)\) and matching powers:

\[\psi_0=1,\qquad \psi_j-\sum_{i=1}^{\min(p,j)}\phi_i\psi_{j-i}=\theta_j\quad(j\ge1),\]

Here set \(\theta_j=0\) for \(j>q\). This explains where the weights \(\psi_j\) come from.

Invertibility: recovering the shocks

We observe \(X_t\), but the shocks \(\varepsilon_t\) are unobserved. To recover a shock, isolate \(\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t\), then undo the MA filter. Assume \(X_t\) is stationary and all MA roots \(w_i\) lie strictly outside the unit circle. Then \(\Theta(L)^{-1}\) has a convergent expansion using current and past observations.

\[\boxed{\Theta(w_i)=0\implies |w_i|>1\quad\text{for every MA root}.}\]\[\boxed{\varepsilon_t=m_X^*+\frac{\Phi(L)}{\Theta(L)}X_t,}\]\[\boxed{m_X^*=-\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}=-\frac{\Phi(1)}{\Theta(1)}m_X.}\]

The constant \(m_X^*\) removes the contribution of the nonzero observation mean after inversion. The superscript star is just a label for this new constant; it is not a mean of \(X_t\). Under the additional causal condition, the recovered shocks are the innovations, as established in Part 3.

1. Start from the ARMA equation

\[\Phi(L)X_t=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

Subtract the intercept from both sides:

\[\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t=\Phi(L)X_t-\mu.\]

2. Apply the inverse MA filter

All MA roots are outside the unit circle, so \(\Theta(L)^{-1}\) is a convergent filter in non-negative powers of \(L\). Apply it to both terms on the right. Linearity gives

\[\varepsilon_t=\frac1{\Theta(L)}\Phi(L)X_t-\frac1{\Theta(L)}\mu.\]

3. Work out what the filter does to the constant

A constant has the same value at every date, so lagging it does not change it:

\[L\mu=\mu,\qquad L^j\mu=\mu.\]\[\begin{aligned}\Theta(L)\mu&=\mu+\theta_1L\mu+\cdots+\theta_qL^q\mu\\&=(1+\theta_1+\cdots+\theta_q)\mu\\&=\Theta(1)\mu.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(1\) is not an MA root, \(\Theta(1)\ne0\). The constant whose image under \(\Theta(L)\) equals \(\mu\) is therefore \(\mu/\Theta(1)\):

\[\Theta(L)\left(\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}\right)=\Theta(1)\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}=\mu,\]\[\frac1{\Theta(L)}\mu=\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}.\]

4. Identify the recovered-shock constant

\[\varepsilon_t=\frac{\Phi(L)}{\Theta(L)}X_t-\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}.\]\[m_X^*=-\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}.\]

The mean equation gives \(\mu=\Phi(1)m_X\). Substitute this identity:

\[m_X^*=-\frac{\Phi(1)m_X}{\Theta(1)}=-\frac{\Phi(1)}{\Theta(1)}m_X.\]

When the MA polynomial has a causal inverse, the unobserved shock can be expressed using current and past observations. The constant is necessary because those observations need not have mean zero.

Recap of conditions

The supplied recap states the following three rules. The last uses a broader meaning of invertibility than the causal inversion just derived.

Stationarity

\[\text{All AR roots: }|z_i|\ne1.\]

A stationary solution exists, possibly using future shocks.

Stability

\[\text{All AR roots: }|z_i|>1.\]

A stationary solution uses current and past shocks only.

Invertibility — wording of the recap

\[\text{All MA roots: }|w_i|\ne1.\]

A two-sided inverse is possible; it may require future observations.

Two meanings of invertibility

The detailed inversion definition requires \(|w_i|>1\), whereas the recap writes \(|w_i|\ne1\). A root strictly inside the circle has an inverse expansion in leads, just as an AR factor does. Excluding the circle therefore allows a bilateral inverse. Recovering shocks using only current and past observations requires the stronger causal invertibility condition \(|w_i|>1\). The two conditions are not interchangeable.

Part 3

Representations

Fundamental representation

Consider again \(\Phi(L)X_t=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t\). The idea of a fundamental representation is that the stationary process contains enough information in its present and past observations to recover the driving shocks. The MA requirement is causal invertibility: every MA root lies outside the unit circle.

AR condition in the supplied definition

The definition explicitly states these conditions for every root of the respective polynomial:

\[\text{AR roots: }|z_i|\le1\quad\text{(labelled “stationarity only”)},\]\[\text{MA roots: }|w_i|>1\quad\text{(invertibility)}.\]

The AR inequality is inconsistent with the stationary-solution theorem: \(|z_i|=1\) is excluded, while roots with \(|z_i|>1\) can also give stationary solutions. It also conflicts with calling the stable AR(1) below “causal and fundamental”.

The stated inequality is preserved here, but it should not be used as a stationarity rule. The coherent distinction is stationarity, \(|z_i|\ne1\), together with recoverability, \(|w_i|>1\). Whether the stationary solution is also causal is a separate question.

A solution involving future shocks can still allow those shocks to be recovered from observations. Recoverability alone does not establish that the shocks are one-step prediction errors.

Causal and fundamental representation

Both directions now use the present and the past: observations can be constructed from shocks, and shocks can be recovered from observations. The definition requires stability in the AR part and causal invertibility in the MA part:

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\Phi(z_i)=0&\implies |z_i|>1\quad\text{for every AR root},\\\Theta(w_i)=0&\implies |w_i|>1\quad\text{for every MA root}.\end{aligned}}\]

The first condition gives a convergent MA(∞) filter. The second gives the convergent inverse filter. Under the no-common-root assumption, neither requirement can be bypassed by cancelling a shared factor.

Proposition: innovations and infinite representations

Assume the representation is causal and fundamental. Then \(\{\varepsilon_t\}_{t\in\mathbb Z}\) is the innovation process of \(X_t\): each shock is the part of the current observation that cannot be predicted linearly from past observations.

There exist real coefficient sequences \(\{a_k\}_{k\ge0}\) and \(\{b_k\}_{k\ge0}\) giving two convergent representations:

MA(∞): observations as weighted shocks

\[\boxed{X_t=m_X+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.}\]

The coefficient \(a_k\) is the weight of the shock \(k\) periods ago in today’s observation. These are the coefficients of \(\Theta(L)/\Phi(L)\), so \(a_k=\psi_k\) in the notation of Part 2 and \(a_0=1\).

AR(∞): shocks recovered from observations

\[\boxed{\varepsilon_t=m_X^*+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_kX_{t-k}.}\]

The coefficient \(b_k\) is the weight of the observation \(k\) periods ago in the recovered shock. Expand \(\Phi(L)/\Theta(L)\) to find these coefficients; \(b_0=1\). Moving the lagged terms to the other side gives the usual autoregressive form:

\[X_t=-m_X^*-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_kX_{t-k}+\varepsilon_t.\]

The AR coefficients in this rearranged equation are \(-b_k\). Both forms describe the same process; one explains an observation using shocks, while the other extracts its current shock from observations.

1. Expand each valid inverse filter

\[\frac{\Theta(L)}{\Phi(L)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_kL^k,\qquad \frac{\Phi(L)}{\Theta(L)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_kL^k.\]

The root conditions make both coefficient sequences absolutely summable. The constant terms of \(\Phi\) and \(\Theta\) are 1, so matching the coefficient of \(L^0\) gives \(a_0=b_0=1\). Applying the filters yields the two displayed representations.

2. Separate the predictable part

\[X_t=m_X+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

Invertibility makes every past shock \(\varepsilon_{t-k}\) a convergent linear combination of observations dated \(t-k\) and earlier. Thus the sum over \(k\ge1\) can be predicted linearly from the observation past.

Causality makes each past observation a combination of past shocks. White-noise orthogonality therefore makes \(\varepsilon_t\) uncorrelated with every past observation. It is precisely the part left over after the best linear prediction.

\[\varepsilon_t=X_t-\widehat X_{t\mid t-1},\qquad \widehat X_{t\mid t-1}=m_X+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

Here \(\widehat X_{t\mid t-1}\) denotes the best linear predictor using all observations before date \(t\).

3. Recover the AR form and its intercept

\[\varepsilon_t=m_X^*+b_0X_t+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_kX_{t-k},\]\[X_t=-m_X^*-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_kX_{t-k}+\varepsilon_t.\]

Because \(\sum_{k\ge0}b_k=\Phi(1)/\Theta(1)\), taking the mean of the inverted equation gives zero: \(m_X^*+m_X\Phi(1)/\Theta(1)=0\). This is the same constant derived in Part 2.

Example: AR(1)

Consider the zero-intercept model below, with independent Gaussian shocks. We want to determine when a stationary solution exists and when that solution is causal.

\[(1-\rho L)X_t=\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2).\]

The AR polynomial is \(\Phi(z)=1-\rho z\). Solving \(1-\rho z=0\) gives \(z_1=1/\rho\) when \(\rho\ne0\). Since \(|z_1|=1/|\rho|\),

\[\text{Stationarity: }|z_1|\ne1\iff|\rho|\ne1,\]\[\text{Stability: }|z_1|>1\iff|\rho|<1.\]

If \(\rho=0\), the equation is simply \(X_t=\varepsilon_t\), with no AR root to check. It is stationary and causal.

Case 1: |ρ| < 1 — stationary and causal

The weights \(1,\rho,\rho^2,\ldots\) shrink in magnitude. A convergent geometric expansion uses only current and past shocks. Since \(\Theta(L)=1\), shock recovery is automatic, so this solution is also fundamental.

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\frac1{1-\rho L}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^kL^k,\]\[\boxed{X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^k\varepsilon_{t-k}.}\]

Write \(X_t=\rho X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), then substitute the equation for the preceding observation:

\[\begin{aligned}X_t&=\rho(\rho X_{t-2}+\varepsilon_{t-1})+\varepsilon_t\\&=\varepsilon_t+\rho\varepsilon_{t-1}+\rho^2X_{t-2}.\end{aligned}\]

After \(n+1\) substitutions,

\[X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^k\varepsilon_{t-k}+\rho^{n+1}X_{t-n-1}.\]

The stationary solution has mean zero and finite variance. The remainder has second moment \(\rho^{2n+2}E(X_t^2)\), which tends to zero for \(|\rho|<1\). The partial sums therefore converge in mean square to the displayed MA(∞) solution.

Case 2: |ρ| > 1 — stationary and noncausal

The lag expansion would use growing weights and cannot converge. Instead, use the lead operator \(F=L^{-1}\), where \(F^k\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t+k}\). The weights \(\rho^{-k}\) now shrink, so a stationary solution can be built from future shocks:

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{-k}F^k,\]\[\boxed{X_t=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{-k}\varepsilon_{t+k}.}\]

This is the source’s fundamental but noncausal case: \(\varepsilon_t=X_t-\rho X_{t-1}\) can still be recovered from observations, but \(X_t\) uses future shocks. The innovation proposition above requires causality as well and cannot be applied to these driving shocks.

1. Factor the operator using a lead

Since \(LF=1\), expand the following product to verify the factorisation:

\[-\rho L(1-\rho^{-1}F)=-\rho L+LF=1-\rho L.\]

2. Invert the factors

\[\frac1{1-\rho L}=-\rho^{-1}F\frac1{1-\rho^{-1}F}.\]

Now \(|\rho^{-1}|<1\). Use the convergent geometric series in \(F\):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac1{1-\rho L}&=-\rho^{-1}F\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{-j}F^j\\&=-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{-(j+1)}F^{j+1}\\&=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{-k}F^k.\end{aligned}\]

3. Apply the inverse to the shocks

\[X_t=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{-k}\varepsilon_{t+k}.\]

The squared weights have finite sum: \(\sum_{k\ge1}\rho^{-2k}<\infty\). White-noise orthogonality makes the variance finite and the series mean-square convergent.

4. Check that it solves the original equation

\[\begin{aligned}\rho X_{t-1}&=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{1-k}\varepsilon_{t-1+k}\\&=-\varepsilon_t-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\rho^{-j}\varepsilon_{t+j}\\&=-\varepsilon_t+X_t.\end{aligned}\]

Rearranging gives \(X_t-\rho X_{t-1}=\varepsilon_t\), as required.

Case 3: |ρ| = 1 — no stationary solution

The root is on the unit circle. Neither the lag expansion nor the lead expansion has decaying weights, so neither yields a finite-variance stationary solution. The process is neither stable nor stationary.

If a stationary finite-variance solution existed, repeated substitution would give

\[X_t-\rho^{n+1}X_{t-n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

For \(|\rho|=1\), the right side has variance \((n+1)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), because different shocks are uncorrelated and each squared coefficient is 1. This grows without bound.

The left side has variance at most \(4\operatorname{Var}(X_t)\): both observations have the same finite variance and their covariance cannot exceed it in absolute value. This bound does not depend on \(n\), which contradicts the growing variance on the right.

Example: MA(1)

Consider a weighted sum of two independent Gaussian shocks:

\[X_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1},\qquad \varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2).\]

The MA polynomial is \(\Theta(z)=1+\theta z\). The equation \(1+\theta z=0\) gives \(z_1=-1/\theta\) for \(\theta\ne0\). This root controls inversion, not stationarity: a finite MA is stationary for every finite \(\theta\).

\[\boxed{\text{Stationary for every finite }\theta.}\]\[\boxed{\text{Causally invertible: }|z_1|>1\iff|\theta|<1.}\]

When \(|\theta|<1\), inversion uses shrinking weights on current and past observations:

\[\varepsilon_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-\theta)^kX_{t-k}.\]

For \(|\theta|\ge1\), this causal convergent expansion is unavailable; the finite MA(1) remains stationary. If \(\theta=0\), then \(X_t=\varepsilon_t\), so recovery is immediate and there is no MA root to check.

Write \(X_t=(1+\theta L)\varepsilon_t\). Replace \(r\) by \(-\theta\) in the geometric identity:

\[(1+\theta L)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-\theta)^kL^k=1-(-\theta)^{n+1}L^{n+1}.\]

For \(|\theta|<1\), the last term vanishes in mean square when applied to a stationary finite-variance process. Thus

\[(1+\theta L)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-\theta)^kL^k,\]\[\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_t&=(1+\theta L)^{-1}X_t\\&=X_t-\theta X_{t-1}+\theta^2X_{t-2}-\cdots.\end{aligned}\]

Separate the current observation and move the lagged terms to the other side:

\[X_t=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-\theta)^kX_{t-k}+\varepsilon_t.\]

This is the AR(∞) representation of the same invertible MA(1). The original finite MA equation and this infinite AR equation describe the same observations.

Part 4

Properties of the Autocorrelation Function

Autocorrelation measures linear dependence between observations separated by \(h\) periods. For a stationary process with positive variance, define

\[\gamma_X(h)=E[(X_t-m_X)(X_{t-h}-m_X)],\]\[\rho_X(h)=\frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)},\qquad \rho_X(0)=1.\]

The numerator is the autocovariance; the denominator is the variance. We calculate positive lags and use \(\rho_X(-h)=\rho_X(h)\) for negative lags. In the source formulas that use \(\mu_X\) for the mean, \(\mu_X=m_X\); it is not the intercept \(\mu\).

ACF of an MA(q) process

Assume the stationary process has a fundamental MA\((q)\) representation. Set \(\theta_0=1\) to include the current shock in the same sum:

\[X_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j},\qquad \widetilde X_t=\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Only shocks with the same date contribute to covariance. At lag zero, each shock is paired with itself. At a positive lag, only the overlapping shock dates remain. This gives

\[\gamma_X(0)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j^2,\]\[\gamma_X(h)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_j\theta_{j+h}\quad(1\le h\le q).\]
\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_j\theta_{j+h}}{\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j^2},&1\le h\le q,\\[8pt]0,&h>q.\end{cases}}\]

The noise variance cancels when we divide by \(\gamma_X(0)\). For \(h>q\), the two observations share no shock dates, so the ACF is exactly zero: it cuts off after lag \(q\).

At \(h=q\), the numerator has just the term \(\theta_0\theta_q=\theta_q\). Symmetry extends the result to negative lags:

\[\rho_X(q)=\frac{\theta_q}{\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j^2},\qquad \rho_X(-h)=\rho_X(h).\]

1. Expand the variance

\[\begin{aligned}\gamma_X(0)&=E\left[\left(\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^q\theta_k\varepsilon_{t-k}\right)\right]\\&=\sum_{j=0}^q\sum_{k=0}^q\theta_j\theta_k E(\varepsilon_{t-j}\varepsilon_{t-k}).\end{aligned}\]

For \(j\ne k\), different dates give expectation zero. For \(j=k\), the expected square is \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Hence

\[\gamma_X(0)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j^2.\]

2. Match dates at a positive lag

\[\widetilde X_{t-h}=\sum_{k=0}^q\theta_k\varepsilon_{t-h-k},\]\[\gamma_X(h)=\sum_{j=0}^q\sum_{k=0}^q\theta_j\theta_k E(\varepsilon_{t-j}\varepsilon_{t-h-k}).\]

The dates must be equal for a term to survive:

\[t-j=t-h-k\iff j=h+k.\]

Since \(j\le q\), this requires \(k\le q-h\). For \(1\le h\le q\), the surviving pairs are \(k=0,\ldots,q-h\):

\[\gamma_X(h)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{k=0}^{q-h}\theta_{h+k}\theta_k.\]

For \(h>q\), there is no allowed \(k\), so the covariance is zero.

3. Divide by the variance

\[\rho_X(h)=\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{k=0}^{q-h}\theta_{h+k}\theta_k}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j^2}=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{q-h}\theta_{h+k}\theta_k}{\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j^2}.\]

At \(h=q\), only \(k=0\) remains. For symmetry, swap the observations in the covariance and shift both dates by \(h\):

\[\begin{aligned}\gamma_X(-h)&=\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t+h})\\&=\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t+h},X_t)\\&=\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})=\gamma_X(h).\end{aligned}\]

Dividing both sides by the same variance gives autocorrelation symmetry.

ACF of a stationary causal AR(p)

The causal assumption is needed here: past observations use past shocks, so they are uncorrelated with the current shock. Write the centred AR equation as

\[X_t-m_X=\phi_1(X_{t-1}-m_X)+\cdots+\phi_p(X_{t-p}-m_X)+\varepsilon_t.\]

To measure dependence at lag \(h\ge1\), multiply by \(X_{t-h}-m_X\) and take expectations. Each lagged observation contributes an autocovariance; the current-shock term vanishes. Therefore

\[\gamma_X(h)=\phi_1\gamma_X(h-1)+\cdots+\phi_p\gamma_X(h-p),\qquad h\ge1.\]

Dividing every term by the positive variance \(\gamma_X(0)\) gives the Yule–Walker recurrence:

\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\phi_1\rho_X(h-1)+\cdots+\phi_p\rho_X(h-p),\quad h\ge1.}\]

1. Multiply by the lagged centred observation

\[\widetilde X_t=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\widetilde X_{t-i}+\varepsilon_t,\]\[\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t-h}=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\widetilde X_{t-i}\widetilde X_{t-h}+\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_{t-h}.\]

2. Take expectations and identify the lags

\[E(\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t-h})=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_iE(\widetilde X_{t-i}\widetilde X_{t-h})+E(\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_{t-h}).\]

The dates \(t-i\) and \(t-h\) are separated by \(h-i\). By stationarity their covariance is \(\gamma_X(h-i)\), including negative lags handled by symmetry.

3. Explain the zero cross-product

Causality gives \(\widetilde X_{t-h}=\sum_{k\ge0}\psi_k\varepsilon_{t-h-k}\). Every shock on the right has a date strictly before \(t\), because \(h\ge1\). Thus

\[E[\varepsilon_t(X_{t-h}-m_X)]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_k E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-h-k})=0.\]

Every expectation in the sum is zero by white-noise orthogonality. Mean-square convergence justifies passing to the limit of the finite sums; independence is not required.

4. Normalise each covariance

\[\gamma_X(h)=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\gamma_X(h-i),\]\[\frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)}=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\frac{\gamma_X(h-i)}{\gamma_X(0)},\]\[\rho_X(h)=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\rho_X(h-i).\]

What the AR recurrence implies

For \(h<p\), some terms have negative lags. Replace them using \(\rho_X(-h)=\rho_X(h)\). Together with \(\rho_X(0)=1\), these equations determine the initial autocorrelations.

For \(h\ge p\), the ACF follows a homogeneous difference equation of order \(p\): each correlation is a fixed linear combination of the preceding \(p\). The same roots that govern the AR dynamics therefore govern the ACF.

Under the stationary causal assumptions, the inverse AR roots have modulus below 1. Their powers shrink, so the ACF tails off toward zero. Positive real roots give non-oscillating exponential components; negative roots can alternate signs; complex roots can generate damped oscillations.

Try a component of the recurrence of the form \(\rho_X(h)=Cr^h\), with a constant weight \(C\) and nonzero \(r\). Substituting and cancelling \(Cr^{h-p}\) gives

\[Cr^h=\phi_1Cr^{h-1}+\cdots+\phi_pCr^{h-p},\]\[r^p=\phi_1r^{p-1}+\cdots+\phi_p,\]\[r^p\Phi(1/r)=0.\]

This is the inverse AR characteristic equation. For distinct roots, the solution combines terms \(C_ir_i^h\). Repeated roots introduce factors such as \(h r_i^h\); these still tend to zero when \(|r_i|<1\).

If \(r_i<0\), its powers change sign. If a conjugate pair is written \(r_i=R e^{\pm i\omega}\), the real combination oscillates while its size shrinks with \(R^h\), where \(R<1\). This explains alternating and damped-oscillating ACF shapes.

ACF of a stationary causal ARMA(p,q)

An ARMA model has lagged shocks on its right-hand side. At early lags, some of those shocks also occur in the earlier observation, so their covariance contributes an extra term. Start from

\[\Phi(L)(X_t-m_X)=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\]\[\widetilde X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_k\varepsilon_{t-k}.\]

The \(\theta_j\) are the original finite MA coefficients, with \(\theta_0=1\). The \(\psi_k\) are the weights in the infinite causal MA representation \(\Theta(L)/\Phi(L)\), with \(\psi_0=1\). The factor \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) is the variance contributed by one matching shock.

For positive lags, the ACF has the following two cases. The first line corresponds to the source’s \(h\le q\) case; lag zero is separately defined by \(\rho_X(0)=1\).

\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned}&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\rho_X(h-i)\\&\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\gamma_X(0)}\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_{j+h}\psi_j\end{aligned},&1\le h\le q,\\[12pt]\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\rho_X(h-i),&h>q.\end{cases}}\]

The correction term records common shocks and divides their covariance contribution by the observation variance. If \(h>q\), even the oldest shock in the finite MA part is more recent than \(X_{t-h}\). Causality makes the overlap impossible, so the direct MA contribution disappears.

\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\phi_1\rho_X(h-1)+\cdots+\phi_p\rho_X(h-p),\quad h>q.}\]

Beyond the MA order, the ACF follows the AR recurrence. For a mixed ARMA model it tails off, with its long-run decay governed by the AR roots; the MA part affects the early correlations and therefore the starting values.

1. Expand the centred model

\[\widetilde X_t=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\widetilde X_{t-i}+\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Multiply by \(\widetilde X_{t-h}\), take expectations and use stationarity for the AR terms:

\[\gamma_X(h)=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\gamma_X(h-i)+\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_jE(\varepsilon_{t-j}\widetilde X_{t-h}).\]

2. Expand the earlier observation into shocks

\[\widetilde X_{t-h}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_k\varepsilon_{t-h-k},\]\[E(\varepsilon_{t-j}\widetilde X_{t-h})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_kE(\varepsilon_{t-j}\varepsilon_{t-h-k}).\]

Only equal dates contribute: \(t-j=t-h-k\), so \(k=j-h\). This index is available only when \(j\ge h\). Consequently,

\[E(\varepsilon_{t-j}\widetilde X_{t-h})=\begin{cases}\sigma_\varepsilon^2\psi_{j-h},&j\ge h,\\0,&j<h.\end{cases}\]

3. Sum the matching shocks

For \(1\le h\le q\), the surviving MA indices are \(j=h,\ldots,q\):

\[\gamma_X(h)=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\gamma_X(h-i)+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=h}^q\theta_j\psi_{j-h}.\]

Rename \(\ell=j-h\). The lower limit becomes 0 and the upper limit becomes \(q-h\):

\[\sum_{j=h}^q\theta_j\psi_{j-h}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{q-h}\theta_{\ell+h}\psi_\ell.\]

For \(h>q\), no index \(j\in\{0,\ldots,q\}\) satisfies \(j\ge h\). The entire MA sum is zero.

4. Divide by the observation variance

\[\rho_X(h)=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\rho_X(h-i)+\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\gamma_X(0)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{q-h}\theta_{\ell+h}\psi_\ell\quad(1\le h\le q).\]

For \(h>q\), only the first sum remains. Renaming the dummy index \(\ell\) as \(j\) gives the displayed two-case formula.

The Yule–Walker matrix system

For a stationary causal AR\((p)\), collect the recurrence equations for \(h=1,\ldots,p\). The unknown coefficients form the vector \(\boldsymbol\phi=(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_p)^\mathsf T\). The right-hand vector \(\boldsymbol r_p\) contains the first \(p\) autocorrelations; here \(\boldsymbol r_p\) is a vector, not an inverse root.

\[\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)&\cdots&\rho_X(p-1)\\\rho_X(1)&1&\cdots&\rho_X(p-2)\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\\rho_X(p-1)&\rho_X(p-2)&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}}_{R_p}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\phi_1\\\phi_2\\\vdots\\\phi_p\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\phi}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\\\vdots\\\rho_X(p)\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol r_p}.\]
\[\boxed{R_p\boldsymbol\phi=\boldsymbol r_p.}\]

The entry in row \(h\), column \(i\) is \(\rho_X(h-i)=\rho_X(|h-i|)\). The diagonal is 1 and every parallel diagonal has the same value: this is a symmetric Toeplitz autocorrelation matrix.

Write the first two recurrence equations and put the coefficients of each AR parameter in the same column:

\[\rho_X(1)=\phi_1\rho_X(0)+\phi_2\rho_X(-1)+\cdots+\phi_p\rho_X(1-p),\]\[\rho_X(2)=\phi_1\rho_X(1)+\phi_2\rho_X(0)+\cdots+\phi_p\rho_X(2-p).\]

Use \(\rho_X(0)=1\) and replace negative lags by their positive counterparts. The first row of coefficients is \((1,\rho_X(1),\ldots,\rho_X(p-1))\); the second begins \((\rho_X(1),1,\ldots)\).

Continue through \(h=p\). Row \(h\) is exactly the equation \(\sum_{i=1}^p\rho_X(h-i)\phi_i=\rho_X(h)\). Stacking the rows produces \(R_p\boldsymbol\phi=\boldsymbol r_p\), with no new assumption beyond the causal Yule–Walker recurrence.

Example: the AR(2) Yule–Walker system

Consider the zero-intercept stationary AR(2), using the causal assumptions of the recurrence above. We want its first two autocorrelations and then all later lags:

\[X_t=\phi_1X_{t-1}+\phi_2X_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t.\]

The \(h=1\) and \(h=2\) equations form the system

\[\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phi_1\\\phi_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\end{bmatrix}.\]

The first row uses \(\rho_X(-1)=\rho_X(1)\); the second uses \(\rho_X(0)=1\). Solving the first row for \(\rho_X(1)\), then substituting it into the second, gives

\[\boxed{\rho_X(1)=\frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2},}\]\[\boxed{\rho_X(2)=\frac{\phi_1^2}{1-\phi_2}+\phi_2.}\]

These values start the second-order recurrence. From lag 3 onward, each new correlation uses the preceding two:

\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\phi_1\rho_X(h-1)+\phi_2\rho_X(h-2),\quad h\ge3.}\]

1. Solve the lag-one equation

\[\rho_X(1)=\phi_1\rho_X(0)+\phi_2\rho_X(-1),\]\[\rho_X(1)=\phi_1+\phi_2\rho_X(1),\]\[\rho_X(1)-\phi_2\rho_X(1)=\phi_1,\]\[(1-\phi_2)\rho_X(1)=\phi_1,\]\[\rho_X(1)=\frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2}.\]

Division is valid in the stable AR(2) case. Its inverse roots satisfy \(|r_1|,|r_2|<1\) and \(r_1r_2=-\phi_2\), so \(|\phi_2|<1\) and \(1-\phi_2\ne0\).

2. Substitute into the lag-two equation

\[\begin{aligned}\rho_X(2)&=\phi_1\rho_X(1)+\phi_2\rho_X(0)\\&=\phi_1\left(\frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2}\right)+\phi_2\\&=\frac{\phi_1^2}{1-\phi_2}+\phi_2.\end{aligned}\]

3. Continue recursively

\[\begin{aligned}\rho_X(3)&=\phi_1\rho_X(2)+\phi_2\rho_X(1)\\&=\phi_1\left(\frac{\phi_1^2}{1-\phi_2}+\phi_2\right)+\phi_2\frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2},\end{aligned}\]\[\rho_X(4)=\phi_1\rho_X(3)+\phi_2\rho_X(2).\]

The same two-term calculation continues for every subsequent lag. The ACF generally decays rather than becoming exactly zero after lag two.

Part 5

Properties of the Partial Autocorrelation Function

Partial autocorrelation and linear projection

The PACF asks how much a lag adds after the intermediate lags have already been used. Work with a zero-mean process, or replace \(X_t\) below by \(\widetilde X_t=X_t-m_X\). Project the current observation onto its first \(h\) lags:

\[X_t=a_{h1}X_{t-1}+\cdots+a_{hh}X_{t-h}+u_t^{(h)}.\]

The coefficients minimise the expected squared prediction error. The residual \(u_t^{(h)}\) is uncorrelated with each included lag. The first subscript records how many lags are in the projection; the second records which lag the coefficient multiplies.

\[\boxed{\alpha_X(h)=a_{hh}.}\]

The PACF at lag \(h\) is the coefficient on the last included lag. It measures the additional linear dependence at that lag after controlling for lags \(1,\ldots,h-1\). These finite-projection coefficients need not equal the infinite-AR coefficients \(-b_k\).

PACF of an AR(p) process

For the stationary causal AR\((p)\), the first \(p\) lags already contain the entire linear predictive part of the model:

\[X_t=\phi_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pX_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t.\]

The remaining shock is orthogonal to all past observations. Including a lag beyond \(p\) therefore adds no predictive information once the first \(p\) lags are controlled for. This gives the source’s cutoff result:

\[\boxed{\alpha_X(h)=\begin{cases}a_{hh},&1\le h\le p,\\0,&h>p.\end{cases}}\]

The PACF cuts off after lag \(p\). At the full order, \(a_{pp}=\phi_p\ne0\). At shorter projection orders, the coefficients may differ from the model coefficients because some relevant lags have not yet been included.

1. Include at least the p model lags

For \(h>p\), consider the candidate projection coefficients

\[(a_{h1},\ldots,a_{hh})=(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_p,0,\ldots,0).\]

Substituting them into the projection leaves exactly \(u_t^{(h)}=\varepsilon_t\).

2. Check the projection conditions

A least-squares linear projection requires the residual to be orthogonal to every regressor. Causality and white-noise orthogonality give

\[E(u_t^{(h)}X_{t-j})=E(\varepsilon_tX_{t-j})=0,\qquad j=1,\ldots,h.\]

These are precisely the normal equations for the projection. To see why their solution is unique, take any nonzero linear combination of the lagged observations. Its most recent observation with a nonzero coefficient contains a current shock that cannot be cancelled by the older observations. That shock has positive variance, so the combination cannot have zero variance. The lag covariance matrix is therefore nonsingular, and the projection coefficients are unique.

3. Read the last coefficient

\[a_{hh}=0\quad(h>p),\qquad\alpha_X(h)=0\quad(h>p).\]

At \(h=p\), the last coefficient is \(\phi_p\). If \(h<p\), omitted model lags can affect the projection, so use \(a_{hh}\) rather than automatically substituting \(\phi_h\).

PACF of an MA(q) process

Let the stationary process have a fundamental MA\((q)\) representation, with \(\theta_0=1\):

\[X_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Its ACF stops after \(q\) because distant observations have no shock in common. Its PACF answers a different question: the additional dependence after controlling for the intermediate observations. Conditioning on those observations changes the comparison, so a zero ACF does not force a zero PACF.

Causal inversion generally writes an MA process as an infinite AR. Distant observations can therefore continue to help the linear prediction, with diminishing effects as the lag grows. Under the fundamental MA assumptions, the PACF tails off toward zero:

\[\boxed{\alpha_X(h)\longrightarrow0\quad\text{as }h\to\infty.}\]

Tailing off means approaching zero, generally without a finite cutoff. The infinite-AR representation explains why no finite AR order truncates the predictive dependence; it does not identify a finite-projection PACF coefficient with an inverse-filter coefficient.

PACF of a causal and fundamental ARMA(p,q)

For a mixed ARMA\((p,q)\) with no common roots, stability gives a causal MA expansion and invertibility gives an infinite AR expansion. The finite MA part does not remove the propagation through the AR terms, and the MA inverse generally keeps infinitely many AR terms.

Consequently neither correlation function generally has a finite cutoff. Under the stated causal and fundamental assumptions, the PACF tails off, just as the ACF does:

\[\boxed{\alpha_X(h)\longrightarrow0\quad\text{as }h\to\infty.}\]

The AR-only and MA-only cases retain their respective cutoff patterns. The mixed-model statement assumes both components are present after any common factors have been removed.

ACF and PACF identification

Cuts off after an order means exactly zero at every larger lag. Tails off means tends to zero as the lag increases, without generally becoming identically zero beyond a fixed lag. These are population properties under the stationarity, causality and invertibility assumptions stated above.
