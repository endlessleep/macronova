Build the model, check its roots, then read its dependence through the ACF and PACF.

01 · The model

Past observations and past shocks

Combine the AR and MA parts

An ARMA(p,q) process combines dependence on past values of the process (the AR part) and current and past white-noise shocks (the MA part). For a weak white noise \(\varepsilon_t\), the general model is

\(X_t=\mu+\phi_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pX_{t-p}\)\({}+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.\)
\[\boxed{\Phi(L)X_t=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t}\]
\[\Phi(L)=1-\phi_1L-\cdots-\phi_pL^p,\]\[\Theta(L)=1+\theta_1L+\cdots+\theta_qL^q.\]

The degrees are \(p\) and \(q\), with \(\phi_p\neq0\) and \(\theta_q\neq0\). The symbol \(\mu\) is the constant in the equation, not necessarily the unconditional mean \(E(X_t)\).

AR(p): no lagged shocks

\[X_t=\mu+\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_iX_{t-i}+\varepsilon_t\]\[\boxed{\Phi(L)X_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t}\]

The MA polynomial reduces to \(\Theta(L)=1\).

MA(q): no lagged observations

\[X_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\]\[\boxed{X_t=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t}\]

The AR polynomial reduces to \(\Phi(L)=1\).

Centre the process around its mean

For a stationary ARMA process, the mean is constant. Take expectations in the model above: the shocks have zero mean, so only the constant and the AR terms remain.

\[m_X=\mu+\left(\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\right)m_X\]\[\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\right)m_X=\mu.\]
\[\boxed{m_X=E(X_t)=\frac{\mu}{1-\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i}=\frac{\mu}{\Phi(1)}}\]

Set \(\widetilde X_t=X_t-m_X\). Substituting \(X_{t-j}=\widetilde X_{t-j}+m_X\) cancels the constant:

\[\widetilde X_t=\phi_1\widetilde X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_p\widetilde X_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.\]
\[\boxed{\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\qquad\widetilde X_t=X_t-m_X.}\]

For an MA(q), \(\Phi(1)=1\) and thus \(m_X=\mu\). For an AR(p) or ARMA(p,q), the equation's constant \(\mu\) and the mean \(m_X\) generally differ.

Related: Lecture 2 · ARMA & lag operators Lecture 2 · Lag-polynomial inversion.

ARMA representation and lag notation

An ARMA\((p,q)\) model has the form

\(X_t=c+\phi_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pX_{t-p}\) \({}+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}\) \({}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.\)

In lag-polynomial notation,

\[\Phi(L)X_t=c+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\]
\[\Phi(L)=1-\phi_1L-\cdots-\phi_pL^p,\]\[\Theta(L)=1+\theta_1L+\cdots+\theta_qL^q.\]

The AR terms move to the left, giving minus signs in the AR polynomial. The MA polynomial retains the plus signs shown in the model.

Lag, difference and lead

\[LX_t=X_{t-1},\qquad L^kX_t=X_{t-k}.\]

The lag operator shifts the date backward. The first difference subtracts the previous observation:

\[\Delta X_t=X_t-X_{t-1}\]\[=X_t-LX_t=(1-L)X_t,\]\[\Delta=1-L.\]

The lead operator shifts the date forward and is the inverse of the lag operator:

\[FX_t=X_{t+1},\qquad F=L^{-1}.\]

Lag-polynomial algebra and first-order inversion

\[P(L)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_kL^k\]

Applying the polynomial shifts each observation by the corresponding number of periods:

\[P(L)X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_kX_{t-k}.\]

For example,

\[(1-\phi L)X_t=X_t-\phi X_{t-1}.\]

First-order inversion

For \(|\rho|<1\), the geometric expansion gives the causal stable inverse:

\[(1-\rho L)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^kL^k.\]

It uses current and past values only.

For \(|\rho|>1\), a stable noncausal inverse exists and involves leads. For \(|\rho|=1\), there is no absolutely summable stable inverse.

02 · Root conditions

Stationarity, stability and invertibility

Stationarity asks whether a stationary solution exists. Stability asks whether it uses only present and past shocks. Invertibility asks whether those shocks can be recovered from observations.

Write \(z_i\) for the roots of \(P(z)=0\) and \(r_i=1/z_i\) for the inverse roots. The unit circle separates roots with modulus below 1 from those above 1.

Stationarity: does a stationary solution exist?

Let \(\mathcal C=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}\) be the complex unit circle. Assume \(\Phi\) and \(\Theta\) have no common roots. A stationary solution exists precisely when none of the roots of the AR polynomial lies on this circle.

\[\boxed{\Phi(z_i)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |z_i|\ne1\ \text{for every AR root}}\]
\[\boxed{r^p\Phi(r^{-1})=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |r_i|\ne1.}\]

For stationarity alone, a characteristic root may be inside or outside the unit circle, but not exactly on it. This is broader than the stability condition below.

Write the stationary solution

Under the hypotheses above, with \(\varepsilon_t\) a weak white noise, define \(\Psi(L)=\Theta(L)/\Phi(L)\). The unique stationary solution is:

\[\boxed{X_t=m_X+\Psi(L)\varepsilon_t,\qquad \Psi(L)=\frac{\Theta(L)}{\Phi(L)},\qquad m_X=\frac{\mu}{\Phi(1)}.}\]

When the solution is stationary but not causal, the Laurent expansion can involve leads and lags:

\[\Psi(L)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_jL^j.\]

A finite-order MA(q) is stationary for any finite MA coefficients when driven by weak white noise: it does not require AR stability roots because \(\Phi(L)=1\).

Stability: build the process from past shocks

The model is stable when every root of the AR characteristic polynomial lies strictly outside the unit circle. This is equivalent to every inverse AR root lying strictly inside it:

\[\boxed{|z_i|>1\ \Longleftrightarrow\ |r_i|<1\quad\text{for all AR roots}.}\]

Under stability, the rational polynomial \(\Psi(L)=\Theta(L)/\Phi(L)\) has a one-sided lag expansion:

\[\Psi(L)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_jL^j,\]\[\widetilde X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Only \(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_{t-1},\ldots\) appear: no future shocks are needed. Thus stability implies a stationary causal solution; stationarity alone does not guarantee causality.

Invertibility: recover shocks from observations

Now the relevant polynomial is the MA polynomial \(\Theta(L)\), not \(\Phi(L)\). When all its characteristic roots are outside the unit circle, the shocks can be recovered using current and past observations:

\[\boxed{\Theta(z_i)=0,\quad |z_i|>1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |r_i|<1.}\]

From \(\Phi(L)X_t=\mu+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t\), isolate the shock:

\[\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t=\Phi(L)X_t-\mu\]\[\varepsilon_t=\frac{\Phi(L)}{\Theta(L)}X_t-\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}.\]
\[\boxed{\varepsilon_t=m_X^*+\frac{\Phi(L)}{\Theta(L)}X_t,\qquad m_X^*=-\frac{\mu}{\Theta(1)}=-\frac{\Phi(1)}{\Theta(1)}m_X.}\]

The inverse \(1/\Theta(L)\) is expanded in non-negative powers of \(L\) to use current and past \(X\)'s. Compare this with stability, which allows \(X_t\) to be expressed using past shocks.

Which roots should you check?

AR roots determine stationarity and stability. MA roots determine invertibility.

Keep the two inequalities separate: \(|z_i|\ne1\) excludes roots on the unit circle; \(|z_i|>1\) places every root strictly outside it. To recover shocks from current and past observations, use the second condition for the MA roots.

Stationarity, moments and covariance

Strong stationarity

A process \((X_t)_{t\in\mathbb Z}\) is strongly (or strictly) stationary if shifting every date by the same amount leaves the entire joint distribution unchanged:

\((X_{t_1},\ldots,X_{t_k})\) \(\overset{d}{=}(X_{t_1+h},\ldots,X_{t_k+h}).\)

This must hold for every collection of dates, every \(k\), and every shift \(h\). The symbol \(\overset{d}{=}\) means “has the same distribution as”. For example,

\((X_t,X_{t+1})\) \(\overset{d}{=}(X_{t+h},X_{t+h+1}).\)

Weak stationarity

A weakly stationary process is also called second-order stationary or covariance-stationary. It satisfies all three conditions:

Constant mean

\[E(X_t)=\mu\]

\(\mu\) does not depend on the date \(t\).

Constant, finite variance

\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\gamma_X(0)<\infty\]

The dispersion is the same at every date.

Covariance depends only on the lag

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})=\gamma_X(h)\]

Only the distance \(h\) matters, not the date \(t\).

Autocovariance symmetry

\[\boxed{\gamma_X(h)=\gamma_X(-h)}\]

Covariance is symmetric. Together with stationarity, this gives

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})=\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t-h},X_t)\]\[=\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t+h}).\]

Weak, strong and Gaussian white noise

Weak white noise

A weak white noise \((\varepsilon_t)\) consists of zero-mean shocks with constant finite variance and no serial correlation:

\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\]\[\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2<\infty,\]\[\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_{t-h})=0\quad(h\ne0).\]
\[\gamma_\varepsilon(h)=\begin{cases}\sigma_\varepsilon^2,&h=0,\\0,&h\ne0.\end{cases}\]

Past shocks contain no linear information about the current shock. This is a statement about correlation, not necessarily independence.

Key rule — match the dates

Since the shocks have zero mean,

\[E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_s)=\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_s)\]
\[\boxed{E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_s)=\begin{cases}\sigma_\varepsilon^2,&t=s,\\0,&t\ne s.\end{cases}}\]

Same date: keep the term. Different dates: the term vanishes.

\[E(\varepsilon_t^2)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\]\[E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1})=0,\]\[E(\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-3})=0.\]

Strong white noise

\[\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}F,\]\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2<\infty.\]

The shocks are independent and identically distributed over time. Independence implies zero covariance, so strong white noise is weak white noise. The converse does not hold.

Gaussian white noise

\[\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2).\]

This is the Gaussian case of strong white noise.

Gaussian WN ⊂ Strong WN ⊂ Weak WN

Calculate roots and inverse roots

\[A(L)=1-a_1L-\cdots-a_pL^p,\]\[A(z)=1-a_1z-\cdots-a_pz^p.\]

There are two equivalent ways to calculate the roots:

Characteristic equation

\[A(z)=0\]

Its roots are \(z_1,\ldots,z_p\).

Inverse characteristic equation

\[B(r)=r^pA(r^{-1})=0\]

Its roots are \(r_1,\ldots,r_p\). The two sets of roots are reciprocals:

\[r_i=\frac1{z_i}.\]

Stability

Every characteristic root must lie outside the unit circle; equivalently, every inverse root must lie inside it:

\[|z_i|>1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|r_i|<1.\]

This condition must hold for every \(i\).

Quadratic roots

For \(az^2+bz+c=0\), with \(a\ne0\),

\[\Delta=b^2-4ac,\]\[z_{1,2}=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}.\]

Here \(\Delta\) is the discriminant. If \(\Delta<0\), the roots are complex conjugates.

03 · Representations

From shocks to observations — and back

Fundamental representation

A representation is fundamental when the model is stationary and its white-noise shocks are recoverable from the present and past of the observed process (the MA component is invertible).

\[\boxed{\text{Stationarity: }\Phi(z_i)=0\Rightarrow|z_i|\ne1,\qquad \text{Invertibility: }\Theta(w_i)=0\Rightarrow|w_i|>1.}\]

Recovering the model’s shocks and constructing the process from past shocks are separate requirements. A stationary AR solution can involve future shocks even when its white-noise shocks can be recovered from observations.

Causal and fundamental representation

The model is causal and fundamental when it is both stable in the AR part and invertible in the MA part.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\text{AR roots: }&\Phi(z_i)=0\ \Rightarrow\ |z_i|>1,\\ \text{MA roots: }&\Theta(w_i)=0\ \Rightarrow\ |w_i|>1.\end{aligned}}\]

Causality means that \(X_t\) can be constructed from present and past shocks. Fundamentality means those shocks can in turn be recovered from current and past observations.

Two ways to write the same process

When the representation is causal and fundamental, \(\{\varepsilon_t\}_{t\in\mathbb Z}\) is the innovation process of \(X_t\), and the following convergent representations exist for suitable coefficient sequences:

\[\boxed{X_t=m_X+\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}a_k\varepsilon_{t-k}}\]\[\text{MA}(\infty)\text{ representation}\]
\[\boxed{\varepsilon_t=m_X^*+\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}b_kX_{t-k}}\]\[\text{AR}(\infty)\text{ / inverted representation}\]

For the MA(∞) form, expand \(\Psi(L)=\Theta(L)/\Phi(L)\): the coefficients tell us how each shock affects the process.

For the inverted form, expand \(\Phi(L)/\Theta(L)\): the coefficients tell us how to recover a shock from observations.

AR(1): three root positions

Consider \((1-\rho L)X_t=\varepsilon_t\), with \(\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2)\). Its characteristic root is \(z_1=1/\rho\) (for \(\rho\ne0\)). The position of this root gives three cases:

\(|\rho|<1\): stationary and stable (causal)

\[\Phi(L)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^kL^k\]\[\boxed{X_t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^k\varepsilon_{t-k}}\]

The process depends on current and past shocks.

\(|\rho|>1\): stationary but not stable (non-causal)

The inverse cannot be expanded as a convergent sum of non-negative powers of \(L\). Using the lead operator \(F=L^{-1}\):

\[\frac1{1-\rho L}=\frac{-\rho^{-1}F}{1-\rho^{-1}F}\]\[=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{-k}F^k\]\[\boxed{X_t=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{-k}\varepsilon_{t+k}}\]

This stationary solution depends on future shocks. This case is why stationarity does not imply stability.

\(|\rho|=1\): no stationary solution

The AR root is on the unit circle, so neither stability nor stationarity holds.

MA(1): when can we recover the shocks?

Consider \(X_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\), with \(\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2)\) and \(\Theta(L)=1+\theta L\). The MA characteristic root is \(z_1=-1/\theta\) for \(\theta\ne0\).

\[\boxed{\text{Stationary for every finite }\theta.}\]\[\boxed{\text{Causally invertible if }|z_1|>1\iff|\theta|<1.}\]

When \(|\theta|<1\), we can recover shocks from present and past \(X\):

\[\varepsilon_t=(1+\theta L)^{-1}X_t\]\[=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-\theta)^kX_{t-k}.\]

At \(|\theta|\ge1\), this one-sided convergent expansion is not available, even though the finite MA(1) remains stationary.

Related: Lecture 2 · Innovation Lecture 2 · Wold decomposition.

Linear prediction and innovations

For a zero-mean weakly stationary process, \(P(X_t\mid H_{t-1})\) is the best linear prediction of \(X_t\) using its past observations. The innovation is the prediction error:

\[\varepsilon_t=X_t-P(X_t\mid H_{t-1}).\]

It is orthogonal to the linear past:

\[E(\varepsilon_tX_{t-k})=0,\qquad k\ge1.\]

Innovations have zero mean and are serially uncorrelated:

\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\]\[E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-k})=0\quad(k\ne0).\]

Wold decomposition

Any zero-mean covariance-stationary process can be decomposed as

\[X_t=\kappa_t+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

Here \(\kappa_t\) is the deterministic component, perfectly predictable from its past. The infinite sum is the nondeterministic component, built from current and past innovations, with \(\psi_0=1\). The deterministic component is uncorrelated with the innovations.

For a purely nondeterministic stationary process, \(\kappa_t=0\):

\[X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

This is an MA\((\infty)\) representation.

From an AR(1) to an infinite MA

An AR(1) is written with its own past values. The aim is to rewrite it using only current and past innovations.

Write the model with \(L\)

\[X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\]\[X_t=\phi LX_t+\varepsilon_t,\]\[(1-\phi L)X_t=\varepsilon_t.\]

Check stability

Before expanding the inverse, use the AR polynomial \(\Phi(L)=1-\phi L\) to find the roots:

\[\Phi(z)=1-\phi z=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z_1=\frac{1}{\phi},\]\[r\Phi(r^{-1})=r(1-\phi r^{-1})=r-\phi=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r_1=\phi.\]

The AR(1) is stable when the characteristic root is outside the unit circle, or equivalently when the inverse root is inside it:

\[|z_1|>1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|r_1|<1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|\phi|<1.\]

Invert and expand

Under this condition, the inverse uses current and past dates only:

\[X_t=(1-\phi L)^{-1}\varepsilon_t.\]
\[(1-\phi L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^jL^j,\]\[X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^jL^j\varepsilon_t.\]

Read the resulting MA\((\infty)\)

Since \(L^j\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_{t-j}\),

\[X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]
\(X_t=\varepsilon_t+\phi\varepsilon_{t-1}\) \({}+\phi^2\varepsilon_{t-2}+\cdots.\)

Related: Lecture 2 · From an MA(1) to an AR(∞).

From an MA(1) to an infinite AR

This is the same method in the other direction. An MA(1) is written with innovations; now the aim is to express it using its own past values and the current innovation.

Write the model with \(L\)

\[U_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1},\]\[U_t=(1+\theta L)\varepsilon_t,\]\[\Theta(L)=1+\theta L.\]

Check invertibility

Before using an infinite series, calculate the inverse root:

\[r\Theta(r^{-1})=r(1+\theta r^{-1})=r+\theta=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r_1=-\theta.\]

The MA(1) is invertible when

\[|r_1|<1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|\theta|<1.\]

Invert and expand

Only under this condition can the polynomial be inverted as a series in current and past values of \(U\):

\[\varepsilon_t=(1+\theta L)^{-1}U_t,\]\[(1+\theta L)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-\theta)^jL^j.\]

Read the resulting AR\((\infty)\)

Apply the series, separate the current value \(U_t\), then move the past terms to the other side:

\[\varepsilon_t=U_t+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-\theta)^jU_{t-j}.\]
\[U_t=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-\theta)^jU_{t-j}+\varepsilon_t.\]
04 · Autocorrelation

ACF of MA(q), AR(p) and ARMA(p,q)

For a stationary process, the ACF is \(\rho_X(h)=\gamma_X(h)/\gamma_X(0)\). What changes between AR, MA and ARMA models is how the autocovariance is obtained and how long it persists.

MA(q): the ACF stops after lag q

For the fundamental MA(q) form \(X_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\), with \(\theta_0=1\), the demeaned observation is \(\widetilde X_t=\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\).

At lag zero, the white-noise cross-products between different dates are zero, leaving only squares:

\[\gamma_X(0)=E\bigl(\widetilde X_t^2\bigr)\]\[=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{q}\theta_j^2.\]

At lag \(h>0\), write \(\widetilde X_{t-h}=\sum_{k=0}^q\theta_k\varepsilon_{t-h-k}\). The shocks match precisely when \(t-j=t-h-k\), or \(j=h+k\). Thus only \(k=0,\ldots,q-h\) can contribute:

\[\gamma_X(h)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{k=0}^{q-h}\theta_{k+h}\theta_k\qquad(1\le h\le q).\]

Divide by \(\gamma_X(0)\):

\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_j\theta_{j+h}}{\sum_{j=0}^{q}\theta_j^2},&1\le h\le q,\\[8pt]0,&h>q.\end{cases}}\]
\[\rho_X(q)=\frac{\theta_q}{\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_j^2},\qquad \rho_X(-h)=\rho_X(h).\]

The ACF cuts off after lag \(q\): there are no common shock dates beyond that point. Also \(\rho_X(0)=1\).

AR(p): the ACF decays gradually

For a stationary causal AR(p), the demeaned process satisfies

\[\widetilde X_t=\phi_1\widetilde X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_p\widetilde X_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t.\]

Multiply only the current equation by \(\widetilde X_{t-h}\) and take expectations:

\[E(\widetilde X_t\widetilde X_{t-h})\]\[=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_iE(\widetilde X_{t-i}\widetilde X_{t-h})+E(\varepsilon_t\widetilde X_{t-h}).\]

For \(h\ge1\), the final term is zero because the current innovation is orthogonal to the past. By stationarity, \(E(\widetilde X_{t-i}\widetilde X_{t-h})=\gamma_X(h-i)\). Therefore

\[\gamma_X(h)=\phi_1\gamma_X(h-1)+\cdots+\phi_p\gamma_X(h-p).\]
\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\phi_1\rho_X(h-1)+\cdots+\phi_p\rho_X(h-p),\qquad h\ge1.}\]

For \(h\lt p\), symmetry \(\rho_X(-h)=\rho_X(h)\) handles negative lags. For \(h\ge p\), this is a homogeneous recurrence of order \(p\). In the causal case the ACF tails off toward zero, possibly alternating or oscillating, instead of cutting off.

ARMA(p,q): what happens after lag q?

Start from \(\Phi(L)\widetilde X_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t\). Under causality, write its MA(∞) form as

\[\widetilde X_t=\Psi(L)\varepsilon_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j},\qquad \Psi(L)=\frac{\Theta(L)}{\Phi(L)}.\]

Multiply the ARMA equation by \(\widetilde X_{t-h}\), and take expectations:

\[\gamma_X(h)=\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\gamma_X(h-i)+\sum_{j=0}^q\theta_jE(\varepsilon_{t-j}\widetilde X_{t-h}).\]

Here the innovation term is different from a pure AR(p): some lagged shocks in \(\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t\) may also occur in \(\widetilde X_{t-h}\). For \(1\le h\le q\), the matching shock contributes \(\psi_{j-h}\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) when \(j\ge h\). For \(h>q\), no MA shock overlaps.

\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\rho_X(h-i)+\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\gamma_X(0)}\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_{j+h}\psi_j,&1\le h\le q,\\[12pt]\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^p\phi_i\rho_X(h-i),&h>q.\end{cases}}\]

New consequence: beyond \(q\), the ACF follows the same recurrence as an AR(p). For a causal and fundamental ARMA(p,q), the ACF generally tails off toward zero, governed in the long run by the AR roots.

\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\phi_1\rho_X(h-1)+\cdots+\phi_p\rho_X(h-p),\quad h>q.}\]

Related: Lecture 2 · MA(1) autocovariance and ACF.

MA(1): calculate the autocovariances

\[U_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1},\qquad E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

The process has mean zero. Write the two observations involved at each lag, expand their product, and keep only products of shocks with the same date.

Lag zero: the variance

\[\gamma_U(0)=E(U_t^2)\]\[=E\!\left[(\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1})^2\right]\]\[=E(\varepsilon_t^2)+2\theta E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1})+\theta^2E(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2)\]\[=(1+\theta^2)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

Lag one: one common shock

\[U_{t-1}=\varepsilon_{t-1}+\theta\varepsilon_{t-2}.\]

The innovation \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\) is the only shock present in both \(U_t\) and \(U_{t-1}\):

\[\gamma_U(1)=E(U_tU_{t-1})\]\[=E\!\left[(\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1})(\varepsilon_{t-1}+\theta\varepsilon_{t-2})\right]\]\[=\theta E(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2)=\theta\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

Lags greater than one: no common shock

For \(h>1\), \(U_t\) uses the shock dates \(t\) and \(t-1\), while \(U_{t-h}\) uses \(t-h\) and \(t-h-1\). No dates coincide, so

\[\gamma_U(h)=0\quad(h>1).\]

Divide by the variance

\[\rho_U(0)=1,\qquad\rho_U(1)=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2},\qquad\rho_U(h)=0\quad(h>1).\]

The ACF of an MA(1) cuts off after lag one.

MA(2): autocorrelations up to lag 3

Question 3 ACF up to lag 3

What to find · Formula

Start from the expected product at each lag. Keep the factor 12 visible in both processes, then divide each autocovariance by the variance.

\[\gamma_Y(h)=E(\widetilde Y_t\widetilde Y_{t-h}),\qquad \rho_Y(h)=\frac{\gamma_Y(h)}{\gamma_Y(0)}.\]

Start from the demeaned process and factor out 12:

\[\widetilde Y_t=12\varepsilon_t-7\varepsilon_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t-2}.\]
\[\boxed{\widetilde Y_t=12\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right).}\]

Since \(E(\widetilde Y_t)=0\), the autocovariance is

\[\boxed{\gamma_Y(h)=E(\widetilde Y_t\widetilde Y_{t-h}).}\]

\(h=0\)

Start from the definition:

\[\gamma_Y(0)=E(\widetilde Y_t\widetilde Y_t)=E(\widetilde Y_t^2).\]

Substitute the factored expression into both copies of \(\widetilde Y_t\):

\[\begin{aligned} \gamma_Y(0)=E\Bigg[&12\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right)\\ &\times12\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right)\Bigg]. \end{aligned}\]

Where does 144 come from? Each process contributes a factor 12, so \(12\times12=144\). This constant can be taken outside the expectation.

\[\gamma_Y(0)=144E\left[\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right)^2\right].\]

Expand the square using

\[(a+b+c)^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2ab+2ac+2bc.\]

Products of innovations from different dates have expectation zero because the innovations are white noise. Only the squared terms contribute:

\[\gamma_Y(0)=144E\left[\varepsilon_t^2+\frac{49}{144}\varepsilon_{t-1}^2+\frac1{144}\varepsilon_{t-2}^2\right].\]

At every date, the innovations have zero mean and variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), so

\[E(\varepsilon_t^2)=E(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2)=E(\varepsilon_{t-2}^2)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\] \[\begin{aligned}\gamma_Y(0)&=144\left(1+\frac{49}{144}+\frac1{144}\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\\&=144\left(\frac{194}{144}\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\gamma_Y(0)=194\sigma_\varepsilon^2.}\]

\(h=1\)

Start again from the definition:

\[\gamma_Y(1)=E(\widetilde Y_t\widetilde Y_{t-1}).\]

Shift every innovation date by one period to write the second process:

\[\widetilde Y_t=12\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right),\] \[\widetilde Y_{t-1}=12\left(\varepsilon_{t-1}-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-3}\right).\]

Substitute both processes:

\[\begin{aligned} \gamma_Y(1)=E\Bigg[&12\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right)\\ &\times12\left(\varepsilon_{t-1}-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-3}\right)\Bigg]. \end{aligned}\]

Again, \(12\times12=144\), so

\[\begin{aligned} \gamma_Y(1)=144E\Bigg[&\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right)\\ &\times\left(\varepsilon_{t-1}-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-3}\right)\Bigg]. \end{aligned}\]

Look for same-date innovations. The two brackets share only \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\) and \(\varepsilon_{t-2}\). Only these two products have nonzero expectations:

\[\left(-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}\right)(\varepsilon_{t-1}),\qquad \left(\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right)\left(-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right).\]
\[\gamma_Y(1)=144E\left[-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}^2-\frac7{144}\varepsilon_{t-2}^2\right].\] \[\begin{aligned}\gamma_Y(1)&=144\left(-\frac7{12}-\frac7{144}\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\\&=144\left(-\frac{84}{144}-\frac7{144}\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2\\&=144\left(-\frac{91}{144}\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\gamma_Y(1)=-91\sigma_\varepsilon^2.}\]

\(h=2\)

Start again from the definition:

\[\gamma_Y(2)=E(\widetilde Y_t\widetilde Y_{t-2}).\]

Shift every innovation date by two periods:

\[\widetilde Y_{t-2}=12\left(\varepsilon_{t-2}-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-3}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-4}\right).\]

Substitute both processes and take \(12\times12=144\) outside the expectation:

\[\begin{aligned} \gamma_Y(2)=144E\Bigg[&\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right)\\ &\times\left(\varepsilon_{t-2}-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-3}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-4}\right)\Bigg]. \end{aligned}\]

There is now only one common innovation: \(\varepsilon_{t-2}\). Its coefficients in the two brackets are \(\frac1{12}\) and \(1\), so

\[\gamma_Y(2)=144E\left[\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}^2\right]=144\left(\frac1{12}\right)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]
\[\boxed{\gamma_Y(2)=12\sigma_\varepsilon^2.}\]

\(h=3\)

Start again from the definition:

\[\gamma_Y(3)=E(\widetilde Y_t\widetilde Y_{t-3}).\]

Shift every innovation date by three periods:

\[\widetilde Y_{t-3}=12\left(\varepsilon_{t-3}-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-4}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-5}\right).\]

Substitute both processes and take \(12\times12=144\) outside the expectation:

\[\begin{aligned} \gamma_Y(3)=144E\Bigg[&\left(\varepsilon_t-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-1}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-2}\right)\\ &\times\left(\varepsilon_{t-3}-\frac7{12}\varepsilon_{t-4}+\frac1{12}\varepsilon_{t-5}\right)\Bigg]. \end{aligned}\]

Compare the innovation dates in the two brackets:

\[\{t,t-1,t-2\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{t-3,t-4,t-5\}.\]

There is no common date, so every product has expectation zero. The same is true at every lag greater than 2:

\[\boxed{\gamma_Y(3)=0,\qquad\gamma_Y(h)=0\quad(h>2).}\]

Convert autocovariances into the ACF

We have \(\gamma_Y(0)=194\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), \(\gamma_Y(1)=-91\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), \(\gamma_Y(2)=12\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), and \(\gamma_Y(h)=0\) for \(h>2\). Divide each autocovariance by \(\gamma_Y(0)\):

\[\rho_Y(h)=\frac{\gamma_Y(h)}{\gamma_Y(0)}.\] \[\rho_Y(0)=\frac{194\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{194\sigma_\varepsilon^2}=1,\] \[\rho_Y(1)=\frac{-91\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{194\sigma_\varepsilon^2}=-\frac{91}{194}\simeq-0.4691,\] \[\rho_Y(2)=\frac{12\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{194\sigma_\varepsilon^2}=\frac{12}{194}=\frac6{97}\simeq0.0619,\] \[\rho_Y(h)=0\qquad(h>2).\]

Calculate the variance, then the autocorrelations

\[X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad|\phi|<1,\]\[\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2).\]

The process has mean zero. To find its ACF, first calculate the variance, which is \(\gamma_X(0)\).

Lag zero: the variance

Square the AR(1) equation and take expectations. The cross term is zero because \(\varepsilon_t\) is uncorrelated with the past value \(X_{t-1}\):

\[\gamma_X(0)=E(X_t^2)\]\[=E\!\left[(\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t)^2\right]\]\[=\phi^2\gamma_X(0)+\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

Move the first term to the left:

\[(1-\phi^2)\gamma_X(0)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\]\[\boxed{\gamma_X(0)=\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2}.}\]

Positive lags: the autocovariance recursion

For \(h\ge1\), multiply the AR(1) equation by \(X_{t-h}\) and take expectations:

\[\gamma_X(h)=E(X_tX_{t-h})\]\[=E\!\left[(\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t)X_{t-h}\right]\]\[=\phi\gamma_X(h-1)+E(\varepsilon_tX_{t-h}).\]

Again, the last term is zero because \(X_{t-h}\) belongs to the past. Hence

\[\gamma_X(h)=\phi\gamma_X(h-1).\]

Applying this recursion repeatedly gives

\[\gamma_X(h)=\phi^h\gamma_X(0)\quad(h\ge0),\]\[\gamma_X(h)=\phi^{|h|}\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2}\quad(h\in\mathbb Z).\]

Divide by the variance

\[\rho_X(h)=\frac{\gamma_X(h)}{\gamma_X(0)}\]\[=\frac{\phi^{|h|}\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)}\]\[=\phi^{|h|}.\]
\[\rho_X(h)=\phi^{|h|}.\]
\[\rho_X(0)=1,\quad\rho_X(1)=\phi,\quad\rho_X(2)=\phi^2.\]

The ACF follows successive powers of \(\phi\); it does not generally cut off after lag one.

05 · Yule–Walker

Yule–Walker equations

The preceding AR(p) recurrence can be written as a matrix system for the AR coefficients \(\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_p\), once the first \(p\) autocorrelations are known.

Put the equations into a matrix

Using \(\rho_X(0)=1\) and the symmetry \(\rho_X(-h)=\rho_X(h)\), put the equations for \(h=1,\ldots,p\) together:

\[\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)&\cdots&\rho_X(p-1)\\\rho_X(1)&1&\cdots&\rho_X(p-2)\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\\rho_X(p-1)&\rho_X(p-2)&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}}_{R_p}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\phi_1\\\phi_2\\\vdots\\\phi_p\end{pmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\phi}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\\\vdots\\\rho_X(p)\end{pmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\rho_p}.\]
\[\boxed{R_p\boldsymbol\phi=\boldsymbol\rho_p.}\]

Each diagonal of \(R_p\) contains the same autocorrelation; this is the Toeplitz structure. The system connects the AR coefficients to the autocorrelations. We will use a related projection system to calculate the PACF.

Work through an AR(2)

Consider \(\widetilde X_t=\phi_1\widetilde X_{t-1}+\phi_2\widetilde X_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t\). For \(h=1,2\):

\[\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_1\\\phi_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\end{pmatrix}.\]

Row one gives \(\phi_1+\phi_2\rho_X(1)=\rho_X(1)\). Move the term in \(\rho_X(1)\) to the left:

\[(1-\phi_2)\rho_X(1)=\phi_1\]\[\boxed{\rho_X(1)=\frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2}.}\]

Row two gives \(\rho_X(2)=\phi_1\rho_X(1)+\phi_2\). Substituting the previous result:

\[\boxed{\rho_X(2)=\frac{\phi_1^2}{1-\phi_2}+\phi_2.}\]

From lag 3 onward, use the original recurrence:

\[\boxed{\rho_X(h)=\phi_1\rho_X(h-1)+\phi_2\rho_X(h-2),\quad h\ge3.}\]

06 · Partial correlation

Recognise a model from its ACF and PACF

The PACF at lag \(h\) is the coefficient on the last lag in the linear projection of \(X_t\) onto \(X_{t-1},\ldots,X_{t-h}\):

\[X_t=a_{h1}X_{t-1}+\cdots+a_{hh}X_{t-h}+u_t^{(h)}\]\[\boxed{\alpha_X(h)=a_{hh}.}\]

AR(p): the PACF cuts off at order p

For a stationary causal AR(p), after controlling for the first \(p\) lags, more distant lags add no further linear predictive information:

\[\boxed{\alpha_X(h)=\begin{cases}a_{hh},&1\le h\le p,\\0,&h>p.\end{cases}}\]

The cutoff is a property of the PACF, not the ACF. In the AR(2) from PS2, the ACF may remain nonzero at \(h=3\), while the PACF is exactly zero for \(h>2\).

MA(q): the PACF tails off

For a fundamental MA(q), the ACF cuts off after \(q\), but the PACF generally does not. Instead, it approaches zero as \(h\to\infty\). The finite MA structure concerns common shocks, not an exact cutoff in partial correlations.

\[\boxed{\alpha_X(h)\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }h\to\infty\qquad\text{(generally no finite cutoff).}}\]

ARMA(p,q): both functions tail off

For a causal and fundamental ARMA(p,q), the ACF and PACF both tend to zero without generally displaying a finite cutoff at lag \(p\) or \(q\).

ACF and PACF patterns by model
ModelACFPACF
AR(p)Tails offCuts off after \(p\)
MA(q)Cuts off after \(q\)Tails off
ARMA(p,q)Tails offTails off

Use these theoretical patterns to distinguish the models: a PACF cutoff points to an AR order, an ACF cutoff points to an MA order, and gradual decay in both suggests a mixed ARMA model.

PACF definition and projection coefficients

The ACF measures the correlation between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-h}\). The PACF measures their linear correlation after controlling for the intermediate lags \(X_{t-1},\ldots,X_{t-h+1}\).

For a zero-mean process, write the projection onto the first \(h\) lags as

\(X_t=a_{h1}X_{t-1}+\cdots\) \({}+a_{hh}X_{t-h}+u_t^{(h)}.\)

The PACF is the coefficient on the last lag:

\[\alpha_X(h)=a_{hh}.\]

For the calculations below,

\[\alpha_X(1)=\rho_X(1),\]\[\alpha_X(2)=\frac{\rho_X(2)-[\rho_X(1)]^2}{1-[\rho_X(1)]^2}.\]

PACF of an AR(1)

The ACF calculation above gives \(\rho_X(1)=\phi\) and \(\rho_X(2)=\phi^2\).

\[\alpha_X(1)=\phi,\]\[\alpha_X(2)=\frac{\phi^2-\phi^2}{1-\phi^2}=0.\]
\(\alpha_X(1)=\phi,\) \(\alpha_X(h)=0\quad(h\ge2).\)

The PACF of an AR(1) cuts off after lag 1.

PACF of an MA(1)

The ACF calculation above gives \(\rho_U(1)=\dfrac{\theta}{1+\theta^2}\) and \(\rho_U(2)=0\).

\[\alpha_U(1)=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2},\]\[\alpha_U(2)=\frac{-\left[\dfrac{\theta}{1+\theta^2}\right]^2}{1-\left[\dfrac{\theta}{1+\theta^2}\right]^2}.\]

Calculate the PACF with Cramer’s rule

\[\boxed{X_t=a_{h1}X_{t-1}+a_{h2}X_{t-2}+\cdots+a_{hh}X_{t-h}+u_t^{(h)}}\]

The PACF at lag \(h\) is the last coefficient in the projection of \(X_t\) on its first \(h\) lags: \(\alpha_X(h)=a_{hh}\).

General autocorrelation matrix and system

The autocorrelation matrix among the lagged values is

\[R_h=\begin{pmatrix} \rho_X(0)&\rho_X(1)&\cdots&\rho_X(h-1)\\ \rho_X(1)&\rho_X(0)&\cdots&\rho_X(h-2)\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ \rho_X(h-1)&\rho_X(h-2)&\cdots&\rho_X(0) \end{pmatrix}.\]

The coefficients satisfy the system \(R_h a_h=\rho_h\), where

\[a_h=\begin{pmatrix}a_{h1}\\a_{h2}\\\vdots\\a_{hh}\end{pmatrix}\]
\[\rho_h=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\\\vdots\\\rho_X(h)\end{pmatrix}.\]

The PACF is the last coefficient, \(a_{hh}\).

Cramer’s rule: replace the last column

For \(a_{hh}\), Cramer’s rule replaces the last column of \(R_h\) with \(\rho_h\):

\[R_h=\begin{pmatrix} \rho_X(0)&\rho_X(1)&\cdots&\rho_X(h-1)\\ \rho_X(1)&\rho_X(0)&\cdots&\rho_X(h-2)\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ \rho_X(h-1)&\rho_X(h-2)&\cdots&\rho_X(0) \end{pmatrix}\]
\[\rho_h=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\\\vdots\\\rho_X(h)\end{pmatrix}.\]

Replace the last column with this vector:

\[R_h^\star=\begin{pmatrix} \rho_X(0)&\rho_X(1)&\cdots&\rho_X(1)\\ \rho_X(1)&\rho_X(0)&\cdots&\rho_X(2)\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ \rho_X(h-1)&\rho_X(h-2)&\cdots&\rho_X(h) \end{pmatrix}.\]

General result

\[\boxed{\alpha_X(h)=a_{hh}=\frac{\det(R_h^\star)}{\det(R_h)}}\]

The denominator uses the original matrix; the numerator uses the matrix with its last column replaced.

Case \(h=2\)

For lag 2,

\[R_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&1\end{pmatrix}\]
\[\rho_2=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(2)\end{pmatrix}.\]

The PACF is \(\alpha_X(2)=a_{22}\), so replace column 2:

\[R_2^\star=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&\rho_X(2)\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[ \alpha_X(2)=\frac{\det(R_2^\star)}{\det(R_2)}= \frac{\begin{vmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&\rho_X(2)\end{vmatrix}} {\begin{vmatrix}1&\rho_X(1)\\\rho_X(1)&1\end{vmatrix}}. \]
\[\det(R_2^\star)=\rho_X(2)-\rho_X(1)^2,\qquad \det(R_2)=1-\rho_X(1)^2.\]
\[\boxed{\alpha_X(2)=\frac{\rho_X(2)-\rho_X(1)^2}{1-\rho_X(1)^2}}\]
