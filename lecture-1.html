LECTURE 1

Introduction to Univariate Time Series

Definitions

Stationarity

Strong stationarity

A process \((X_t)_{t\in\mathbb Z}\) is strongly (or strictly) stationary if shifting every date by the same amount leaves the entire joint distribution unchanged:

\((X_{t_1},\ldots,X_{t_k})\) \(\overset{d}{=}(X_{t_1+h},\ldots,X_{t_k+h}).\)

This must hold for every collection of dates, every \(k\), and every shift \(h\). The symbol \(\overset{d}{=}\) means “has the same distribution as”. For example,

\((X_t,X_{t+1})\) \(\overset{d}{=}(X_{t+h},X_{t+h+1}).\)

Weak stationarity

A weakly stationary process is also called second-order stationary or covariance-stationary. It satisfies all three conditions:

Constant mean

\[E(X_t)=\mu\]

\(\mu\) does not depend on the date \(t\).

Constant, finite variance

\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\gamma_X(0)<\infty\]

The dispersion is the same at every date.

Covariance depends only on the lag

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})=\gamma_X(h)\]

Only the distance \(h\) matters, not the date \(t\).

Autocovariance symmetry

\[\boxed{\gamma_X(h)=\gamma_X(-h)}\]

Covariance is symmetric. Together with stationarity, this gives

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})=\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t-h},X_t)\]\[=\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t+h}).\]
Exercise method

Mean → variance → autocovariance. Check each quantity for dependence on calendar time. If one condition fails, stop: the process is not weakly stationary.

For example, \(E(X_t)=a+bt\) with \(b\ne0\) already rules out stationarity.

Distinction

Strong versus weak stationarity

Strong → weak

Provided the first two moments are finite, strong stationarity implies weak stationarity. The whole distribution is unchanged, so these moments are unchanged too.

Weak ⇏ strong

Weak stationarity controls the mean, variance and autocovariances only. It does not generally imply that the entire joint distribution is unchanged.

Key rule

For a Gaussian process, weak stationarity does imply strong stationarity. Here “Gaussian process” refers to the joint Gaussian distributions of its observations.

Definitions & rules

White noise

Weak white noise

A weak white noise \((\varepsilon_t)\) consists of zero-mean shocks with constant finite variance and no serial correlation:

\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\]\[\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2<\infty,\]\[\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_{t-h})=0\quad(h\ne0).\]
\[\gamma_\varepsilon(h)=\begin{cases}\sigma_\varepsilon^2,&h=0,\\0,&h\ne0.\end{cases}\]

Past shocks contain no linear information about the current shock. This is a statement about correlation, not necessarily independence.

Key rule — match the dates

Since the shocks have zero mean,

\[E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_s)=\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_s)\]
\[\boxed{E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_s)=\begin{cases}\sigma_\varepsilon^2,&t=s,\\0,&t\ne s.\end{cases}}\]

Same date: keep the term. Different dates: the term vanishes.

\[E(\varepsilon_t^2)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\]\[E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1})=0,\]\[E(\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-3})=0.\]

Strong white noise

\[\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}F,\]\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2<\infty.\]

The shocks are independent and identically distributed over time. Independence implies zero covariance, so strong white noise is weak white noise. The converse does not hold.

Gaussian white noise

\[\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2).\]

This is the Gaussian case of strong white noise.

Gaussian WN ⊂ Strong WN ⊂ Weak WN
Example

Uncorrelated does not mean independent

Take \(X\sim N(0,1)\) and \(Y=X^2\). Their covariance is zero:

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\operatorname{Cov}(X,X^2)\]\[=E(X^3)-E(X)E(X^2)\]\[=E(X^3)=0.\]

The last equality uses the zero third moment of a centered normal variable. Yet \(Y\) is completely determined by \(X\), so the two variables are dependent.

Common mistake

Independence implies zero covariance when the moments exist, but zero covariance does not imply independence. Zero covariance excludes linear association as measured by covariance, not every form of dependence.

Formula

Moments & autocovariance

Use these definitions before expanding a variance or covariance. All moments involved must exist.

Mean and variance

\[E(X_t),\]\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=E\!\left[(X_t-E(X_t))^2\right]\]

The König–Huygens identity gives

\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=E(X_t^2)-[E(X_t)]^2.\]

Covariance

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,Y_t)\]\[=E\!\left[(X_t-E(X_t))(Y_t-E(Y_t))\right]\]
\(\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,Y_t)=E(X_tY_t)\) \({}-E(X_t)E(Y_t).\)

Autocovariance

For a weakly stationary process, the covariance at lag \(h\) can be written as a function of \(h\) alone:

\[\gamma_X(h)=\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})\]
\(=E(X_tX_{t-h})\) \({}-E(X_t)E(X_{t-h}).\)
Key rule

For a zero-mean stationary process,

\[\gamma_X(h)=E(X_tX_{t-h}).\]

At lag zero,

\[\gamma_X(0)=\operatorname{Var}(X_t).\]
Exercise tools

Working identities

\[\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X).\]

Variance of a sum

\(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) \({}+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).\)
\(\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\) \({}+b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).\)
Key rule

If the variables are uncorrelated, the covariance term vanishes:

\(\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\) \({}+b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y).\)

Linearity of covariance

\(\operatorname{Cov}(aX+bY,Z)=a\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)\) \({}+b\operatorname{Cov}(Y,Z).\)
\(\operatorname{Cov}(X,aY+bZ)=a\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\) \({}+b\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z).\)

These identities are especially useful for linear combinations of white-noise shocks.

Worked method

Processes built from white noise

Consider the process

\[X_t=a\varepsilon_t+b\varepsilon_{t-1},\]

where \((\varepsilon_t)\) is weak white noise. Apply the same sequence: mean, variance, autocovariance.

Mean

\(E(X_t)=aE(\varepsilon_t)\) \({}+bE(\varepsilon_{t-1})=0.\)

Variance

\(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)\) \({}+b^2\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{t-1})\) \({}+2ab\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_{t-1})\)

The covariance term is zero because the shock dates differ:

\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=(a^2+b^2)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

Autocovariance at lag one

Write the two observations side by side:

\[X_t=a\varepsilon_t+b\varepsilon_{t-1},\]\[X_{t-1}=a\varepsilon_{t-1}+b\varepsilon_{t-2}.\]

Their mean is zero, so

\[\gamma_X(1)=E(X_tX_{t-1})\]\[=E[(a\varepsilon_t+b\varepsilon_{t-1})(a\varepsilon_{t-1}+b\varepsilon_{t-2})].\]

Expand the product:

\(\gamma_X(1)=a^2E(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1})\) \({}+abE(\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-2})\) \({}+abE(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2)\) \({}+b^2E(\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-2}).\)

Only \(E(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) survives:

\[\gamma_X(1)=ab\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

Autocovariance at larger lags

\[X_{t-h}=a\varepsilon_{t-h}+b\varepsilon_{t-h-1}.\]

For \(h>1\), there is no shock date shared by \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-h}\). Every expected cross-product is zero:

\[\gamma_X(h)=0\quad(h>1).\]
\(E(X_t)=0,\) \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=(a^2+b^2)\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\)
\[\gamma_X(1)=ab\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\]\[\gamma_X(h)=0\quad(h>1).\]
Exercise method

Look for shocks appearing in both observations. Only common shock dates can generate a nonzero covariance.

Diagnosis

Non-stationarity & deterministic trend

A process fails covariance stationarity if its mean, variance or covariance varies with calendar time. One failure is enough; there is no need to show that every condition fails.

Deterministic trend

\[X_t=a+bt+\varepsilon_t,\qquad b\ne0.\]
\[E(X_t)=a+bt+E(\varepsilon_t)=a+bt.\]

The mean depends on time, so the process is not weakly stationary. No further calculation is needed.

Detrending

Subtract the deterministic component:

\[\widetilde X_t=X_t-a-bt=\varepsilon_t.\]

The transformed process is white noise and therefore stationary.

Exercise method

For this deterministic trend, remove the deterministic component instead of automatically taking a first difference.

Diagnosis

Random walk & first differences

\[X_t=X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\quad(t\ge1)\]

Here \(X_0\) is fixed and the weak white-noise shocks have variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2>0\). Repeated substitution gives

\(X_t=X_0+\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2\) \({}+\cdots+\varepsilon_t\)
\[=X_0+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_s.\]

Variance

\(X_0\) is fixed, and all covariances between different shock dates are zero:

\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_s\right)\]\[=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_s)\]\[=\underbrace{\sigma_\varepsilon^2+\cdots+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}_{t\text{ terms}}\]\[=t\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

Autocovariance

For \(0

\[X_{t-h}=X_0+\sum_{s=1}^{t-h}\varepsilon_s.\]

Both observations contain \(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_{t-h}\). Each of these \(t-h\) common shocks contributes its variance:

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})=(t-h)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]
\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=t\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\]\[\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-h})=(t-h)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

The variance depends on \(t\), and the covariance depends on both \(t\) and \(h\). The random walk is non-stationary.

First differencing

Subtract the previous observation:

\[\Delta X_t=X_t-X_{t-1}\]\[=X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t-X_{t-1}\]\[=\varepsilon_t.\]

\(X_t\) is non-stationary, but \(\Delta X_t\) is stationary. Its first difference is weak white noise.

Comparison

Choose the transformation

Deterministic trend

\[X_t=a+bt+\varepsilon_t\]

Non-stationarity comes from the deterministic component \(a+bt\).

Transformation

Detrend by subtracting this component.

\[X_t-a-bt=\varepsilon_t.\]

Random walk

\[X_t=X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\]

Non-stationarity comes from the accumulation of random shocks.

Transformation

First-difference the process.

\[\Delta X_t=\varepsilon_t.\]

Both can appear persistent, but the source of their non-stationarity differs. Choose the transformation accordingly.

Definitions

Ergodicity

Ergodicity means that statistics computed from one sufficiently long realization converge to the corresponding population quantities.

Mean ergodicity

\[\bar X_T=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_t,\qquad \mu=E(X_t).\]
\[\bar X_T\xrightarrow{p}\mu=E(X_t).\]

A process is mean ergodic when its sample mean converges in probability, denoted by \(\xrightarrow{p}\), to its population mean.

Compare \(E(X_t)\) with \(\lim_{T\to\infty}\bar X_T\). If they coincide, the process is mean ergodic; if they do not, it is not.

A sufficient condition

\[\boxed{\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}|\gamma_X(h)|<\infty}\]

Absolute summability of the autocovariances is sufficient for mean ergodicity: dependence between observations must disappear sufficiently quickly as the lag increases.

White noise

\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\qquad \bar\varepsilon_T\xrightarrow{p}0.\]
\[\bar\varepsilon_T\xrightarrow{p}E(\varepsilon_t).\]

White noise is stationary and mean ergodic.

A permanent random component

\[X_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t,\]\[\mu\sim U[-1,1],\]\[\varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2).\]

The same random variable \(\mu\) appears at every date. Here \(\mu\) is a random component, not the population mean.

\[\bar X_T=\mu+\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon_t.\]
\[E(X_t)=0,\qquad \bar X_T\xrightarrow{p}\mu.\]

The sample mean converges to the random common component, rather than to the population mean. Therefore

\[\bar X_T\not\xrightarrow{p}E(X_t).\]

The process is stationary but not mean ergodic.

Stationarity versus ergodicity

Stationarity does not imply ergodicity. A process can have time-invariant population moments while the statistics from one realization fail to converge to them.
